# W02 — Brainstorm angles

**Runner:** the model generates, you react. **Reads:** `work/W01_notes_*.md`, `work/W01_cross-readings.md`, `work/W01b_seminar-notes.md`, `context/LL-readings/papers-on-ai-notes.md`. **Writes:** `work/W02_angles.md`.

## Why an angle

A seminar paper lives or dies on its claim, and a claim comes from a lens
held up to a passage. This course supplies its own lenses; the brainstorm
does not need to import any. The point of generating many is to get past
the first one, which is usually the topic announced as a thesis.

## The lenses (the course's own)

- **Prehistory.** "What did these writers get right, and what did they get wrong (so far)?" Shelley, Wilde, Čapek read as forecasts, and the forecast checked against the papers.
- **Dialogue.** The chat interface as the inheritor of Socratic and Confucian dialogue; Grice's maxims and Sacks's turn-taking as the unwritten rules a chatbot keeps or breaks.
- **The fabricated assistant.** Askell et al.'s "helpful, honest, harmless" persona as a made character; who made it, out of what, and what the course's made beings say about that.
- **Role-play.** Shanahan's claim that a model plays a role rather than being a self, set against the portrait, the robot, the creature, the mask.
- **Writing as *pharmakon*.** Thamus's reply to Theuth: a technology that stands in for memory and gives "the appearance of wisdom." The oldest critique of a text-generating tool.
- **The mechanical and the living.** Bergson's "something mechanical encrusted on the living" as the formula for why machines are funny, and whether chatbots are.
- **Imitation.** How a model imitates genres and styles, and what Wilde's "Critic as Artist" already knew about imitation as creation.

## Prompt

> Read the notes and the seminar notes. Produce 8 to 12 candidate angles. For each give:
> 1. **Name** (two to five words).
> 2. **The lens** it holds up, from the list above or one the notes suggest.
> 3. **The texts it collides** (at least one course reading and, where it fits, one AI paper), and the tension that collision produces, with the two passages by location.
> 4. **The claim** in one sentence. A claim, not a topic: something a reader could disagree with.
> 5. **The crux passage**: the one place in the text the whole paper would have to read closely.
> 6. **The counter-reading** a good reader would raise, in one sentence.
> 7. **Why the professor would care / why the professor would object**, one line each, grounded in the syllabus.
> 8. **Fit** 1 to 5: can this be argued in the paper's length from the texts we have?
>
> Then rank the list and defend the top three in a paragraph each. Note which angles you dropped and why.

## Output

A ranked list, the three defended, the drops explained. You choose in `W03`.
