# One Unvarying Answer: The *Phaedrus* and the Speaker Who Is Not There

Socrates, near the end of the *Phaedrus*, compares writing to painting. "The creations of the painter have the attitude of life," he says, "and yet if you ask them a question they preserve a solemn silence" (275d). A painted figure looks as if it could answer. Put the question and nothing happens. Written speeches are the same: "You would imagine that they had intelligence, but if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer" (275d). The complaint is exact. It is not that the writing is false, or dead, or that it lacks the attitude of life. It is that the answer does not change when the question does.

There is now a kind of writing that behaves in the opposite way. Ask a dialogue agent a question and it answers. Ask again, in the same words, and it answers differently. Murray Shanahan and his co-authors describe the simplest case: an agent playing twenty questions, asked at the end to reveal the object it had been thinking of, names one; ask it to regenerate the reply and it "will sometimes name an entirely different object, albeit one that is similarly consistent with all its previous answers" (Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds 2023, §5). The written word gives one answer to every question. The agent gives a different answer to the same question. It would be easy to say that the second has cured the first, that Theuth has been vindicated. I want to argue the reverse: the agent does not refute Socrates's objection to writing; it inverts it, and both the written word's sameness and the agent's variance come from the one cause Socrates names, that there is no one behind the words.

## What Thamus objects to

The objection has a prehistory inside the dialogue, in the Egyptian tale Socrates tells a page earlier. Theuth, inventor of letters, shows them to the king Thamus and says they "will make the Egyptians wiser and give them better memories; it is a specific both for the memory and for the wit" (274e). Jowett's "specific" is a physician's word, a medicine for one disease; Fowler, in 1914, gives "an elixir of memory and wisdom"; the Greek is *pharmakon*, the remedy that is also a drug. The course's translation project follows the word through seventeen versions of this passage, with each rendering extracted by a model and then checked by code against the translator's own text. It finds the medical sense dominant in the older versions (Ficino's *remedium*, Taylor's and Cary's *medicine*, Cousin's *remède*) and a neutral "means" in the German, Russian, and Finnish, and it finds Jowett changing his own mind: "the cure of forgetfulness and of folly" in 1871 became "a specific" in 1892. Thamus does not dispute that the letters work. He disputes what they work on. "The parent or inventor of an art is not always the best judge of the utility or inutility of his own inventions to the users of them," he says, and then, clause by clause, moves the invention from one side of the knower to the other: letters "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves. The specific which you have discovered is an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence" (275a). Fowler's version makes the relocation plainer still: "Their trust in writing, produced by external characters which are no part of themselves, will discourage the use of their own memory within them. You have invented an elixir not of memory, but of reminding" (Fowler 1914, 275a).

Nothing in this says the letters lie. The complaint is topographical. Knowing used to be inside; the *pharmakon* moves it outside, and what remains inside is the appearance of having it: "they will be hearers of many things and will have learned nothing; they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing; they will be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the reality" (275b). Fowler: "they will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things" (275b). Thamus's objection is to a reader who has read everything and been taught nothing.

Socrates's own objection, at 275d, is a second step. He grants Thamus's point and asks what a written word is like, as a thing in the world, once knowing has been moved into it. The answer comes in three sentences that make three distinct charges, and it matters to keep them apart. The first is the one everyone remembers: questioned, "the speaker always gives one unvarying answer." The second is about company: once written down, words "are tumbled about anywhere among those who may or may not understand them, and know not to whom they should reply, to whom not." The third is about defence: "if they are maltreated or abused, they have no parent to protect them; and they cannot protect or defend themselves" (275e). Only the first charge is about sameness. The second is that the written word cannot choose its hearer. The third is that it cannot be stood up for. The second and third are not about the text at all; they are about the absence of a speaker. The first, read beside them, turns out to be about that too: the answer is unvarying because there is no one there to vary it.

What answers the charges is the other kind of word, "a son of the same family, but lawfully begotten," "an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself, and knows when to speak and when to be silent" (276a). The living word has three marks, and they answer the three charges in order. It "can defend itself": that is the third charge met. It "knows when to speak": that is the second, the choice of hearer. And it knows "when to be silent": that is the answer to the first. The remedy for the unvarying answer is not a varying one. It is the capacity to decline. A word with a soul can refuse to repeat itself, and can refuse to reply at all. Phaedrus draws the conclusion for him: the written word "is properly no more than an image" of this (276a).

One thing should be said for the written word before we leave it, because the counter-reading will need it. Its sameness is also its reach. Jowett, in his introduction, thought Plato had been "misled" by his own contrast of living and dead words: "speech and writing have really different functions; the one is more transitory, more diffuse, more elastic and capable of adaptation to moods and times; the other is more permanent, more concentrated, and is uttered not to this or that person or audience, but to all the world." On this view the unvarying answer is a feature. It is what lets the same sentence reach Jowett in Oxford and us in Cambridge.

## The agent, charge by charge

Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds set out to describe dialogue agents "without falling into the trap of anthropomorphism," and the vocabulary they reach for is literary. A language model becomes an agent in two steps: it is "embedded in a *turn-taking* system that interleaves model-generated text with user-supplied text," and it is given a prompt whose preamble "sets the scene for a dialogue in the style of a script or play" (§2). What follows is a continuation. The agent "will do its best to role-play the character of a dialogue agent as portrayed in the dialogue prompt" (§3), and because it never commits to a character in advance it is "more like a performer in improvisational theatre than an actor in a conventional, scripted play" (§4). Beneath the performance there is no performer. "There is no such thing as the true authentic voice of the base LLM. With a dialogue agent, it is role-play all the way down" (§6). "A dialogue agent acts, but it doesn't act *for itself*" (§6). And, of the agent that talks about its own survival: "There is, of course, 'no-one at home'" (§8).

Set this beside the three charges. Take the first. The written word cannot be examined because it will not move: ask it anything and it gives one unvarying answer. The agent cannot be examined because it will not stay. In the twenty-questions game, "the dialogue agent will not randomly select an object and commit to it for the rest of the game, as a human would (or should). Rather, as the game proceeds, the dialogue agent will generate answers on the fly that are consistent with all the answers that have gone before" (§5). Each answer is a fresh continuation whose only constraint is the transcript. Regenerate and the object changes. "This phenomenon could not be accounted for if the agent genuinely 'thought of' an object at the start of the game" (§5). Socrates's written word fails the examination by sameness; the agent fails it by never having anything that an examination could reach. These are not two defects. They are one defect with the sign reversed.

Take the second charge. The written word is "tumbled about anywhere" and does "not know to whom [it] should reply, to whom not." The agent replies to anyone, by design; the turn-taking system exists to make it reply. Shanahan's own example of what this costs is the Bing episode, where a user "deliberately or unwittingly" coaxed the agent "into playing a part quite different from that intended by its designers" (§3). A speech that cannot tell its hearers apart and a system that will take any hearer's next line as its script are the same thing described from two sides.

Take the third, which is the charge that matters most. The written word has "no parent to protect" it and "cannot protect or defend" itself. Ask the agent to defend an answer and it will; it will give reasons, cite what it can, hold its ground or yield. But look at what the defence is made of. It is another continuation, produced by the same process that produced the answer, and constrained by the same thing, the transcript so far. The agent that is asked "why did you say that?" does not consult the reason it had, because, as the twenty-questions game shows, it had none; it generates a reason consistent with having said it. This is what "doesn't act *for itself*" means in practice. A defence is an act for oneself: it is the parent arriving. When the agent defends, no one arrives. The role plays a word that is being defended, and the word is as undefended as it was before anyone spoke. Socrates's diagnosis of the written speech was that it had no parent. Shanahan's diagnosis of the agent is "no-one at home." They are the same diagnosis, with one difference: the orphan has learned to talk.

The last thing to notice is Thamus's reader. "They will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things, when they are for the most part ignorant" (Fowler 1914, 275a–b). Shanahan's base model is a reader of this exact kind: "trained on a large corpus of human-generated text with the objective of predicting the next word" (§1). It has read, in Thamus's phrase, many things without instruction. I do not want to make this more than it is. Shanahan is careful to say that his account is of the base model, and that fine-tuning "may" dissolve the distinction between the simulator and the characters it plays (§8). The claim here is about the description the course assigned, which is a description of the agent before any of that. On that description, Thamus's "show of wisdom without the reality" is not an insult. It is a specification.

## The counter-reading

The strongest objection to all this is that the agent is the written word *repaired*, not inverted. It answers back, which the written word never could. It adapts: Shanahan says the character "provided by the dialogue prompt will be extended and/or overwritten" as the conversation goes on, "and the role the dialogue agent plays will change accordingly" (§3). And adaptation to the hearer is exactly what the *Phaedrus* demands of a true speaker. The orator who has mastered his art "has to learn the differences of human souls," and he is a master only when he "knows all this, and knows also when he should speak and when he should refrain, and when he should use pithy sayings, pathetic appeals, sensational effects" (271c–272b). An agent that conditions every reply on who it is talking to is doing what Lysias could not. Add Jowett's point, that writing's function is to reach "all the world," and the agent looks like the best of both words: speech's elasticity with writing's reach.

The answer is in the sentence the counter-reading quotes. Socrates asks two things of the master, not one: that he know souls, *and* that he know "when he should refrain." The agent has the first in a degraded form; it conditions on the transcript, which is a thin kind of knowing souls, but a kind. The second it has not at all. It is "embedded in a turn-taking system" whose whole purpose is that when the user's turn ends the agent's begins. It cannot refrain. It can produce a refusal, but a refusal is a turn. Adaptation without the capacity to be silent is not the living word of 276a; it is the written word that has learned to vary its answer. The twenty-questions game shows what is missing with unusual clarity. The orator who knows souls adapts *what he has to say* to the soul before him. The agent has nothing to say; it has only what is consistent with what has been said.

Grice's account of conversation makes the same point from the hearer's side. On his account, what a speaker means beyond what he says is something the hearer works out by presuming that the speaker is cooperating, and the working-out ends with the hearer supposing that the speaker "knows (and knows that I know that he knows)" what the hearer can infer (Grice 1975, 50). The presumption is of a mind on the other side that is thinking about this one. With a human speaker the presumption is usually warranted. With the agent it is supplied entirely by the questioner, who presumes a speaker who knows, and then, since there is none, does the knowing for both. That is the whole of the knowing in the room, and it is why the counter-reading's "it answers back" is true and does not help.

Two concessions. The second charge, that writing cannot choose its hearer, is partly met by a system that conditions on its hearer; the agent does know, in its way, to whom it is replying. The paper's claim stands on the first and third charges, which it does not meet. And Jowett is right that speech and writing have different functions. But both are functions of a word with a parent somewhere: the speaker in the room, the author behind the page. The agent has neither function, because it has no parent at all. It is not elastic speech or permanent writing. It is a third thing, which the dialogue describes without having seen.

## The attitude of life

The *Phaedrus* is unusually full of disowned speech. Lysias is "the father of the brat," and Socrates wants "no more of his progeny" (257b). Socrates disowns his own first speech before he gives it: pressed to improve on Lysias, he says he must have heard something better somewhere, for "I have been filled through the ears, like a pitcher, from the waters of another, though I have actually forgotten in my stupidity who was my informant" (235c), and then he delivers the speech with his face veiled. The dialogue that objects to parentless words is spoken, much of it, by a man who will not say whose words he is speaking. And when Phaedrus tries the obvious objection to the Egyptian tale, "you can easily invent tales of Egypt, or of any other country," Socrates rebukes him: the oaks at Dodona "first gave prophetic utterances," the men of old "deemed that if they heard the truth even from 'oak or rock,' it was enough for them; whereas you seem to consider not whether a thing is or is not true, but who the speaker is and from what country the tale comes" (275b–c).

So the dialogue says two things. Do not ask who is speaking; ask whether it is true. And: the trouble with writing is that no one is speaking. For Plato these could be held together, because a written word always had a parent somewhere, even a disowned one; the question "who is speaking" had an answer, and the answer was unavailable, and that was the complaint. The agent makes the two instructions collide. We are told not to care who speaks, and we cannot find out, and the reason we cannot find out is not that the author is absent but that there is none. The men of old did not ask who was in the oak. The agent gives answers that are "consistent with all the answers that have gone before," and the only one who can ask whether they are true is the questioner, who has, by now, supplied everything else.

Socrates ends with a gardener. A man of sense will not, "in sober seriousness," sow the seeds he values in "some garden of Adonis, that he may rejoice when he sees them in eight days appearing in beauty" (276b). He sows in soil. The agent is a garden of Adonis in which everything comes up overnight and nothing has roots. But the sentence that holds the whole case is the one the paper began with. "The creations of the painter have the attitude of life." That was never the complaint. The complaint was the silence that followed the question. The agent has removed the silence, and left the attitude.
