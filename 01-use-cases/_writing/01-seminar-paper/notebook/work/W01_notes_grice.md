## 1. What it is

A lecture in philosophy of language that begins as an intervention in a logicians' quarrel and becomes a theory of how speakers mean more than they say. Its machinery (the Cooperative Principle, four categories of maxims, the notion of flouting, the three-condition definition of conversational implicature) is offered throughout as "a first approximation" with its gaps marked.

## 2. The argument, in order

See the skeleton. In one line: what conversation adds to the meaning of *and*, *or*, *if* is not in the words but in the hearer's presumption that the speaker is cooperating; implicature is what the hearer must suppose to keep that presumption alive when a maxim is visibly broken; and it must be workable-out, cancelable, and carried "by the saying of what is said" (p. 58).

## 4. Where AI enters

- **Dialogue and who is speaking.** The whole apparatus is written from the hearer's side: the general pattern on p. 50 is in the hearer's voice ("He has said that *p*…"). The speaker appears only as what the hearer presumes: cooperative, able to comply, not opting out, expecting to be understood. A chatbot is a speaker about whom every one of those presumptions is in doubt and all of them are made anyway.
- **The fabricated assistant.** The maxims are a list of what a cooperative contributor does: as informative as required, not more; truthful and evidenced; relevant; perspicuous. Grice says the list was written "as if this purpose were a maximally effective exchange of information" and that this "is, of course, too narrow" (p. 47). A system prompt is a list of this kind, written for the speaker rather than presumed by the hearer.
- **Writing as *pharmakon*.** Grice's basis for the maxims is habit: "they have learned to do so in childhood" (p. 48). A learned habit of truth-telling is exactly the kind of thing Thamus says writing will not give.
- **The mechanical.** The four screws and the rubber spoon (p. 47): cooperation measured by whether the thing handed over is the thing needed. The spoon that is not a spoon is a Quality failure in a transaction.
- **Imitation.** Grice's examples of exploitation (irony, metaphor, the testimonial, the tea party) are all cases where the words are a performance the hearer must see through. The hearer's seeing-through is the implicature.

## 5. Tensions with other readings

- **With the *Phaedrus*.** Socrates says written words "preserve a solemn silence" when questioned and "the speaker always gives one unvarying answer" (Jowett; 275d). Grice's implicature requires a speaker who could have said otherwise and is presumed to have chosen. Writing, on Socrates's account, cannot implicate; it can only be interpreted. Yet Grice's own examples include a written testimonial (p. 52) and Blake's lines (p. 54), and the general whom "*Peccavi*" served (pp. 54–55) sent a message. Grice treats writing as speech with an absent speaker presumed; Plato treats it as an orphan.
- **With Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson** (not re-read for this run). Grice has no turn; the exchange is described by contributions. The one place turn-structure appears is the quasi-contract's third feature, "You do not just shove off" (p. 48), which Grice then rejects as a basis.
- **With Bergson** (not re-read). "Be brief (avoid unnecessary prolixity)" (p. 46) and Miss X's "series of sounds" (p. 55): the mechanical repetition that Bergson makes the source of laughter is, for Grice, a flouted maxim of Manner.

## 6. Tensions with the AI papers

- **Shanahan et al. (2023), read in full for this run.** Shanahan's dialogue agent "will do its best to role-play the character of a dialogue agent as portrayed in the dialogue prompt" (§3) and "it is role-play all the way down" (§6). Grice's condition (3) requires that "the speaker thinks (and would expect the hearer to think that the speaker thinks) that it is within the competence of the hearer to work out" the supposition (p. 50). Shanahan would say the agent can role-play a speaker who thinks this; Grice's definition has no clause for role-played thinking. The two texts meet exactly at the parenthesis in condition (3).
- **Shanahan on deception (§7)** distinguishes three ways of being false (making things up; false "in good faith"; "deliberately" deceptive) and says only the first "is directly applicable" to an agent, because an agent has no beliefs or intentions "in a literal sense." Grice's first failure, "quietly and unostentatiously VIOLATE a maxim" (p. 49), is deliberate misleading; his Quality maxims are about belief and evidence. Shanahan's agent cannot violate in Grice's sense; it can only role-play a violator. Whether that difference is visible to the hearer is the question.
- **Askell et al. (2021), not read for this run.** Known for framing the assistant as helpful, honest, harmless; *to verify* against the paper before any citation. The obvious collision is with the four categories on pp. 45–46.

## 7. Questions the text leaves open

1. Can a hearer's presumption of cooperation be rational when the speaker has no history of learning to cooperate (p. 48)?
2. Is opting out ("my lips are sealed," p. 49) available to a speaker who cannot end the exchange?
3. What is the implicature of a refusal, if the refusal is the only move a speaker is permitted?
4. If implicature is "carried … by the saying of what is said" (p. 58), what carries it when the saying is sampled?
5. What does "letting down … himself" (p. 49) mean for a speaker who, in Shanahan's words, "doesn't act *for itself*" (§6)?
