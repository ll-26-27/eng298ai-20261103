# W01 — Cross-readings

Pairs of passages from different texts that sit in tension, each with the
question it generates and the texts a paper would need. From the three texts
read whole for this run (Grice; Shanahan et al.; the *Phaedrus*). Stephanus
numbers for the *Phaedrus* are supplied, not in the Jowett file; verify
against Fowler in the Phaedrus-walk use case.

## 1. One unvarying answer, or a different answer every time

- *Phaedrus* 275d: "if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer."
- Shanahan §5: "Since the object 'revealed' is, in fact, generated on the fly, the dialogue agent will sometimes name an entirely different object, albeit one that is similarly consistent with all its previous answers."

**Question.** Plato's objection to writing is that it cannot answer back; Shanahan's observation about the agent is that it answers back differently each time and never committed to anything. Are these the same defect (no one behind the words) under opposite symptoms, and which is worse for a learner? **Texts:** *Phaedrus* 274c–276a; Shanahan §§4–7.

## 2. Knowing when to be silent

- *Phaedrus* 276a: the living word "knows when to speak and when to be silent."
- Shanahan §2: a dialogue agent is an LLM "embedded in a *turn-taking* system that interleaves model-generated text with user-supplied text"; Grice p. 49: a speaker may "OPT OUT … *I cannot say more; my lips are sealed*"; Grice p. 48: "You do not just shove off."

**Question.** Socrates's test of a word with a soul is that it can keep silent. Grice's speaker can opt out but, in the quasi-contract, cannot leave. The agent must take its turn. Is a speaker that cannot be silent capable of the thing Socrates means by knowing? **Texts:** *Phaedrus* 275d–276a; Grice pp. 48–49; Shanahan §2, §6.

## 3. The hearer's presumption and the role

- Grice p. 50, condition (3): "the speaker thinks (and would expect the hearer to think that the speaker thinks) that it is within the competence of the hearer to work out … the supposition."
- Shanahan §6: "With a dialogue agent, it is role-play all the way down."

**Question.** Implicature needs a speaker presumed to think about the hearer. If there is "no-one at home" (Shanahan §8), the hearer supplies the thinking and attributes it. Is that what Shanahan means by role-play, and is it what Grice means by implicature, or has the hearer become the author? **Texts:** Grice pp. 49–50, 53–54; Shanahan §§3–6.

## 4. Habit versus instruction

- Grice p. 48: people follow the maxims because "they have learned to do so in childhood and not lost the habit of doing so … It is much easier, for example, to tell the truth than to invent lies."
- Shanahan §1: "A human's language skills are an extension of the cognitive capacities they develop through embodied interaction with the world, and are acquired by growing up in a community of other language users"; §3: "commercial enterprises … attempt to give them personas that are friendly, helpful, and polite. This is done partly through careful prompting and partly by fine-tuning."
- *Phaedrus* 275a: letters give "the semblance of truth"; learners "will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves."

**Question.** Grice's basis for cooperation is a childhood; Shanahan's agent has none and gets a persona by prompt and fine-tuning; Thamus says what is supplied from outside produces semblance. Is the assistant's cooperativeness a habit, a rule, or a semblance, and can the hearer tell? **Texts:** Grice pp. 47–49; Shanahan §§1, 3, 8; *Phaedrus* 274e–275b.

## 5. Commonplaces and arrangement

- *Phaedrus* 235e–236a: "These are the commonplaces of the subject which must come in … the only merit is in the arrangement of them, for there can be none in the invention"; 264d, the Midas epitaph where "whether a line comes first or comes last … makes no difference."
- Shanahan §3: "the training set provisions the language model with a vast repertoire of archetypes and a rich trove of narrative structure on which to draw as it 'chooses' how to continue a conversation."

**Question.** Socrates grants that invention is impossible and merit lies in arrangement; then he condemns Lysias for having no arrangement. Shanahan's model has the whole repertoire and continues "in the same vein." Is "carry on in the same vein" arrangement, in Socrates's sense, or the Midas epitaph? **Texts:** *Phaedrus* 235c–236a, 264b–266c; Shanahan §§2–3.

## 6. The inventor is not the best judge

- *Phaedrus* 274e: "the parent or inventor of an art is not always the best judge of the utility or inutility of his own inventions to the users of them."
- Shanahan §10: "It is not within the scope of this paper to provide recommendations"; the paper is by a DeepMind affiliate about systems DeepMind builds.

**Question.** Thamus's principle applied to the papers: the course asks students to read the inventors' own accounts of the assistant. What is the standing of a vocabulary for the machine written by its makers? **Texts:** *Phaedrus* 274c–275b; Shanahan abstract, §1, §10; the affiliation lines of the 22 papers (see the talking-machines map, `02a-affiliations.json`).

## 7. The place of the argument

- *Phaedrus* 230d: "the men who dwell in the city are my teachers, and not the trees or the country. … only hold up before me in like manner a book, and you may lead me all round Attica."
- Grice p. 47: the maxims have "analogues in the sphere of transactions that are not talk exchanges" (mending a car, baking a cake).

**Question.** Socrates leaves the city for a book, and the argument against writing happens where no one lives. Grice's conversation happens anywhere two people share a task. The chat window is nowhere. Does the argument about where speech happens survive a speaker who is in no place? **Texts:** *Phaedrus* 227a–230e, 279b–c; Grice pp. 47–48; the Phaedrus-walk use case for the ground.
