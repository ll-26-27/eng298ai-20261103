## 1. Opening on a passage (~300 words)

- **Claim.** Socrates's objection to writing is that it cannot be questioned; the dialogue agent can be questioned endlessly, and that is its defect.
- **Sources.** 275d–e (Jowett), quoted. Shanahan §5, quoted.
- **Move.** The painted figure that "preserve[s] a solemn silence." The written speech that, asked, "always gives one unvarying answer." Then: ask an agent, and ask again, and it "will sometimes name an entirely different object." The claim as the last sentence: the agent inverts the objection rather than refuting it, and the cause is the one Socrates names.

## 2. The reading (~900 words)

- **2a. Location, not truth** (~350). 274e–275b. Theuth's "specific both for the memory and for the wit" (Fowler: "elixir"); Thamus's reply: "trust to the external written characters," "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence" (Fowler: "of reminding"), "hearers of many things and will have learned nothing," "appear to be omniscient." The word *pharmakon* via the rendering table (Jowett "specific," Fowler "elixir"; the course's translation project follows it across seventeen versions). Point: Thamus never says the letters lie. Knowing has moved outside the knower.
- **2b. Three charges, three marks** ★ (~400). 275d–e: (i) "the speaker always gives one unvarying answer"; (ii) "tumbled about anywhere … know not to whom they should reply, to whom not"; (iii) "no parent to protect them; and they cannot protect or defend themselves." Only (i) concerns sameness. 276a's living word: "can defend itself" answers (iii); "knows when to speak and when to be silent" answers (i) and (ii). Silence is the answer to the unvarying answer: a living word can decline to repeat itself and decline to reply at all.
- **2c. The written word's one virtue** (~150). It is the same for everyone, which Jowett's introduction calls its function: "uttered not to this or that person or audience, but to all the world." Set up here so the counter-reading can use it.

## 3. The collision (~750 words)

- **3a. Shanahan's agent** (~250). §2, §4, §6, §8 paraphrased with the key phrases quoted: "turn-taking system," "script or play," "improvisational theatre," "role-play all the way down," "no-one at home."
- **3b. The inversion, charge by charge** (~350). §5 quoted. (i) The agent's answer never holds: each is "generated on the fly," "consistent with all the answers that have gone before," and nothing else. The written word cannot be examined because it will not move; the agent cannot be examined because it will not stay. (ii) It replies to anyone: 275d's "tumbled about anywhere" as a design. (iii) Its defence of an answer is itself a continuation (§6, "doesn't act for itself"): undefended in Socrates's sense. Cause: "no parent" / "no-one at home."
- **3c. The appearance of wisdom** (~150). Fowler's "read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know many things." The base model as Shanahan describes it. §8's limit stated.

## 4. The counter-reading (~450 words)

- **4a. At full strength** (~200). The agent as the written word repaired: it answers; it adapts (§3); adaptation is what 271c–272b demands; and Jowett says the comparison of speech and writing is unfair in principle, since writing's function is to reach everyone, and the agent has that reach with speech's elasticity.
- **4b. Answer** (~250). 271c–272b asks for knowledge of souls *and* knowledge of "when he should refrain." The agent conditions on its hearer (a degraded form of the first) and cannot refrain (§2). Adaptation without refraining is the written word that has learned to vary, not the living word. The 20-questions test shows what is missing: something to say. Grice p. 50, one sentence: the questioner supplies the "knows (and knows that I know that he knows)," and with the agent that supply is the whole of the knowing. Concede charge (ii) in part. Stand on (i) and (iii). Answer Jowett: his "different functions" are functions of a word with a parent somewhere; the agent has neither function because it has no parent at all.

## 5. Close (~300 words)

- 257b, Lysias "the father of the brat," and 235c, Socrates "filled through the ears, like a pitcher": the dialogue is full of disowned speech, and Socrates's own. Then 275b–c: "you can easily invent tales of Egypt" / "you seem to consider not whether a thing is or is not true, but who the speaker is." The dialogue tells us not to ask who is speaking, in the middle of an argument that no one is. The agent makes the two collide. Garden of Adonis, 276b–c. Return to "the attitude of life." Last sentence about the text.
