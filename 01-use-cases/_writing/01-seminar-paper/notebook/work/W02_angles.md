## 1. One unvarying answer

- **Lens.** Writing as *pharmakon*; dialogue and who is speaking.
- **Collision.** *Phaedrus* 275d ("the speaker always gives one unvarying answer") against Shanahan §5 (regenerate, and the agent "will sometimes name an entirely different object"). Plato's complaint about writing and Shanahan's observation about the agent are mirror images.
- **Claim.** The chatbot does not refute Socrates's objection to writing; it inverts it. The written word fails because it cannot vary; the model fails because it cannot do anything else. Both failures have the same cause, which Socrates names and Shanahan confirms: no one is behind the words.
- **Crux.** 275d–276a, read with 274e–275b: "one unvarying answer," "tumbled about anywhere," "no parent," and the living word that "knows when to speak and when to be silent."
- **Counter-reading.** The agent *does* answer back; it adapts to the questioner (Shanahan §3, the role refined by the conversation), which is what Socrates asked of the living word and the orator who knows souls (271c–272b). On this reading the chatbot is the written word repaired. The paper must show that adaptation without commitment is not what "knows when to speak" means.
- **Why the professor would care.** It is the syllabus's own assignment: "*Phaedrus*, esp. 257c onward." **Would object.** If the paper treats Thamus's speech as a prophecy rather than a position Socrates argues for; Phaedrus's "you can easily invent tales of Egypt" (275b) is in the text for a reason.
- **Fit.** 5.

## 2. Role-play all the way down, and the hearer's presumption

- **Lens.** Role-play; dialogue.
- **Collision.** Grice's condition (3) (p. 50), the parenthesis "(and would expect the hearer to think that the speaker thinks)," against Shanahan §6, "it is role-play all the way down," and §8, "no-one at home."
- **Claim.** Conversational implicature, which needs a speaker presumed to think about the hearer, is exactly what a hearer supplies when talking to an agent, and that supply is what Shanahan calls role-play seen from the other side: the hearer, not the model, is the one playing a part.
- **Crux.** Grice pp. 49–50 and the general pattern on p. 50; the irony example on p. 53 with its three layers of mutual knowledge.
- **Counter-reading.** The garage (p. 51): implicature is worked out from remark, context, and presumption; the speaker's nature never enters. If the hearer does the work, nothing changes when the speaker is a model. Answer with the layered knowledge on p. 50.
- **Why the professor would care.** Seminar 7's question verbatim: "how does a system that predicts continuations create the impression of a character or self?" Here the answer is: by the hearer's Gricean habit. **Would object.** Grice is one week's reading; the paper must not become philosophy of language with the literature left out. Bring the *Phaedrus* in at the close.
- **Fit.** 5.

## 3. The maxims written down

- **Lens.** The fabricated assistant.
- **Collision.** Grice p. 48 (the maxims as childhood habit; the rationalist wish for a basis) against Shanahan §3 (personas "friendly, helpful, and polite … partly through careful prompting and partly by fine-tuning") and Askell et al. (*to verify*: helpful, honest, harmless).
- **Claim.** The assistant persona is the Cooperative Principle made explicit and imposed from outside, and the writing-down changes what the maxims are: from a hearer's presumption into a speaker's instruction.
- **Crux.** Grice pp. 47–49: "too narrow," the "dull" answer, the rationalist wish, the failed quasi-contract.
- **Counter-reading.** Grice's maxims were always normative ("something that it is REASONABLE for us to follow," p. 48); writing them as instructions is what Grice wanted. The paper must show that reasonableness-for-us is not the same as a rule-for-it.
- **Why the professor would care.** The fabricated persona is the course's thesis for Seminar 7. **Would object.** Askell et al. has not been read whole; without it this is a sketch.
- **Fit.** 3 now; 4 after a `W01` on Askell.

## 4. Knowing when to be silent

- **Lens.** Dialogue; the mechanical.
- **Collision.** *Phaedrus* 276a (the living word "knows when to speak and when to be silent") against Shanahan §2 (the turn-taking system) and Grice pp. 48–49 (opting out; "You do not just shove off").
- **Claim.** Socrates's criterion for a word with a soul is the capacity for silence; the chat interface removes that capacity by design, so an agent's refusal is never silence but always speech, and reads as Grice's opting out, which is the one failure that cannot generate meaning without becoming a flout.
- **Crux.** 276a with 275d; Grice p. 49, "my lips are sealed."
- **Counter-reading.** Socrates himself cannot be silent in this dialogue; Phaedrus extracts a speech by oath (236d–e) and the sign forbids him to leave (242b–c). Silence is never available to the Platonic Socrates either. The paper must distinguish refusal from silence.
- **Why the professor would care.** Dialogue as form, with the interface as the new constraint. **Would object.** Needs Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson for turn-taking, not read.
- **Fit.** 3.

## 5. Commonplaces and arrangement

- **Lens.** Imitation; authorship.
- **Collision.** *Phaedrus* 235e–236a ("the only merit is in the arrangement of them, for there can be none in the invention") and 264d (the Midas epitaph) against Shanahan §§2–3 ("carry on in the same vein"; "a vast repertoire of archetypes").
- **Claim.** Socrates's theory of composition, which denies invention and locates merit in arrangement, is the theory a language model satisfies, and the Midas epitaph is Socrates's name for what it produces when the arrangement is only local.
- **Crux.** 264b–266c: the living creature, the epitaph, collection and division "where the joint is."
- **Counter-reading.** Socrates does not mean it; "I have been filled through the ears, like a pitcher" (235c) is irony, and the palinode is invention. The paper has to decide how seriously to take Socrates's disclaimers of authorship, which Jowett already flags.
- **Why the professor would care.** "How it imitates existing genres and styles" is in the course description. **Would object.** Risk of a general claim about creativity with the passage left behind.
- **Fit.** 4.

## 6. The inventor is not the best judge

- **Lens.** Prehistory; the fabricated assistant.
- **Collision.** *Phaedrus* 274e (Thamus's principle) against the fact that the course's AI papers are written by the systems' makers (Shanahan at DeepMind; the affiliation lines in the talking-machines map).
- **Claim.** The course asks students to learn what the assistant is from papers written by its inventors; Thamus's principle says the inventor is the wrong judge; the *Phaedrus* therefore supplies the method for reading the AI papers, which is to read them as Theuth's speech and supply Thamus's reply.
- **Crux.** 274d–275b, the exchange's structure: inventor's claim, king's judgment.
- **Counter-reading.** Shanahan's paper is itself a Thamus-reply (its whole aim is to resist the inventors' anthropomorphism). The paper must sort the papers.
- **Why the professor would care.** A method for the "papers-on-ai" folder the syllabus assigns. **Would object.** It could become a survey; it needs one paper read closely, not twenty-two glanced at.
- **Fit.** 3.

## 7. Where the argument happens

- **Lens.** Dialogue; the setting as a condition of speech.
- **Collision.** *Phaedrus* 230d (Socrates leaves the city for a book) and 279b–c (the return) against the placelessness of the chat window; Grice's car and cake (p. 47) as talk tied to a shared task in a place.
- **Claim.** The *Phaedrus* makes its argument about writing in the one place Socrates does not belong, and walks home; a chatbot speaks from nowhere; the paper reads the dialogue's topography as part of its claim about what a word needs in order to live.
- **Crux.** 227a–230e and 279b–c, with the Phaedrus-walk use case for the ground.
- **Counter-reading.** Jowett: the scene is "to preserve the scene in the recollection of the reader," ornament not argument.
- **Why the professor would care.** It uses the lab's own map. **Would object.** Literary-topographical, lighter on the AI question.
- **Fit.** 3.

## 8. The wolf and the lamb

- **Lens.** Prehistory; the mask.
- **Collision.** Socrates's veiled first speech (237a; "As wolves love lambs," 241d) and the palinode against Shanahan §8–§10 on the agent that role-plays self-preservation and the "rogue AI" trope.
- **Claim.** The *Phaedrus* stages, in Socrates's two speeches, the thing Shanahan fears: a speaker who takes a part from a repertoire, says what the part says, and must recant because the part was impious. The dialogue's remedy is the palinode, which the agent does not have.
- **Crux.** 242b–243b, the sign and the recantation.
- **Counter-reading.** Socrates recants because he believes Eros is a god; the recantation has a ground; an agent's "correction" has none. That may be the point rather than the objection.
- **Why the professor would care.** It is about persona as performance. **Would object.** Ingenious; the link to Shanahan is analogy.
- **Fit.** 3.

## The top three, defended

**Angle 1, "One unvarying answer."** This is the paper the syllabus points at. The crux passage is the one the course assigns, the collision text is the course's required AI reading, and the claim is a reading of the passage, not a thesis laid over it: Socrates says written words always answer the same; the agent never does; both because no one is there. It has a real counter-reading (the agent as the living word repaired, since it adapts to the hearer) that the paper can answer from 276a, where the test is not adaptation but knowing "when to be silent." It needs no text not already read whole. And it closes the circle the course draws from the Socratic dialogue to the chat window.

**Angle 2, "Role-play all the way down."** The sharpest claim of the eight: that Gricean implicature with a machine is the hearer playing a role. It answers Seminar 7's question in the seminar's own terms. Its risk is that it becomes philosophy of language; the remedy is to open on Grice's tea party (p. 54) and close on the *Phaedrus* (275d), so the literature frames the argument. Also fully in the bank.

**Angle 5, "Commonplaces and arrangement."** The most literary of the three and the one that engages the course's "imitates existing genres and styles" directly. Its crux (264c–d, the living creature and the Midas epitaph) is vivid, and the counter-reading (Socrates's irony about his own invention) is a genuine interpretive problem, which is a virtue in a seminar paper. Slightly less certain in its collision: Shanahan's "carry on in the same vein" is one sentence, and the paper would want one of the technical papers (the few-shot paper, *to verify*) beside it.
