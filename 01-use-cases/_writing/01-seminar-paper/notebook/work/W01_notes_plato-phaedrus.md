## 1. What it is

A dialogue in two movements, set outside the city walls on the Ilissos under a plane tree. The first movement is three speeches on love: Lysias's written speech, read aloud by Phaedrus (the non-lover should be preferred); Socrates's first speech, veiled, in the same vein; and Socrates's palinode, the myth of the soul as charioteer and the praise of divine madness. The second movement is a conversation about speaking and writing: what rhetoric is, what dialectic is, and the myth of Theuth and Thamus on the invention of letters, with the judgment that the written word is an image of the living word "graven in the soul." It ends with a prayer to Pan and "Let us go."

Jowett's introduction argues the dialogue has not one subject but several (love, rhetoric, dialectic, writing) held together by rhetoric as "the continuous thread," and that Lysias's speech is probably Plato's own invention.

## 2. The argument or action, in order

1. **Outside the wall (227a–230e).** Phaedrus comes from Lysias at the house near the Olympieion; Socrates detects the roll under his cloak; they walk up the Ilissos barefoot; Boreas and Oreithyia; Socrates declines the rationalizers' allegories because he "must first know myself"; the plane tree, the agnus castus, the spring, the cicadas; Socrates admits "the men who dwell in the city are my teachers, and not the trees."
2. **Lysias's speech (230e–234c).** Read from the book. The non-lover's case: no repentance, no neglect of affairs, more of them to choose from, less gossip, fewer quarrels.
3. **Socrates's first speech (237a–241d).** Veiled. Defines love as "the irrational desire which overcomes the tendency of opinion towards right"; the lover keeps the beloved ignorant, soft, friendless, and poor; "As wolves love lambs so lovers love their loves." Breaks off before the praise of the non-lover.
4. **The sign and the palinode (242a–257b).** Noon; the daimonic sign forbids him to leave; the two speeches were impious; Stesichorus's recantation; the four madnesses (prophetic, purificatory, poetic, erotic); the soul immortal as self-mover; the charioteer and two horses; the procession of the gods and the place beyond heaven; recollection; the growth of wings at the sight of beauty; the taming of the black horse; the lover's and beloved's life.
5. **Writing and the politicians (257c–258e).** Phaedrus fears Lysias will be called a "speech writer"; Socrates: the politicians write laws and want immortality by authorship; "there is no disgrace in the mere fact of writing. … The disgrace begins when a man writes not well, but badly."
6. **The cicadas (258e–259d).** Once men who died singing; they report to the Muses who honours them; "we ought always to talk and not to sleep at mid-day."
7. **True and false rhetoric (259e–274b).** Must the speaker know the truth? The ass called a horse; rhetoric as "a universal art of enchanting the mind by arguments"; deception works by small resemblances; Lysias's speech "begins at the end," no definition, no order, like the Midas epitaph; a discourse "ought to be a living creature"; collection and division, "where the joint is"; the catalogue of rhetoricians' terms; preliminaries mistaken for the art (medicine, tragedy, harmony); rhetoric like medicine must know the soul; Tisias's probability; "a man of sense should not try to please his fellow-servants … but his good and noble masters."
8. **Theuth and Thamus (274c–275b).** The Egyptian god shows the king his inventions; letters "will make the Egyptians wiser and give them better memories; it is a specific both for the memory and for the wit"; Thamus: the inventor is not the best judge; letters "will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters"; "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence"; "they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing."
9. **Writing like painting (275c–276a).** Phaedrus: "you can easily invent tales of Egypt"; Socrates: the oak and the rock; written words "preserve a solemn silence" when questioned; "the speaker always gives one unvarying answer"; "tumbled about anywhere"; "no parent to protect them." The other word: "an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself, and knows when to speak and when to be silent," of which "the written word is properly no more than an image."
10. **The gardens of Adonis (276b–277a).** The sensible husbandman will not sow in earnest in a hot-bed for an eight-day show; writing is "for the sake of recreation and amusement," "memorials … against the forgetfulness of old age"; the dialectician sows words "which are able to help themselves," with "a seed which others brought up in different soils render immortal."
11. **Conclusion (277a–279c).** Summary of the conditions of art; writing with the pretence of "certainty and clearness" is a disgrace; the right man values the word in his own bosom and its "lawful descendants" in others; the message to Lysias, Homer, Solon: if they can defend their writings by spoken argument they deserve the name "lovers of wisdom"; the prophecy for Isocrates; the prayer to Pan; "Let us go."

## 4. Where AI enters

- **Writing as *pharmakon*.** The oldest critique of a technology that stands in for memory and produces "the semblance of truth" (275a). Every clause of Thamus's reply has a present referent: "trust to the external written characters" (retrieval); "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence" (context versus training); "hearers of many things and will have learned nothing"; "appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing"; "the show of wisdom without the reality." The course's syllabus points students to 257c onward for exactly this reason.
- **Dialogue and who is speaking.** The living word "knows when to speak and when to be silent" (276a); the written word "always gives one unvarying answer" (275d) and "know[s] not to whom they should reply, to whom not." A chatbot is a text that replies to anyone and never keeps silent, which is the written word's defect made worse; or a text that gives varying answers, which is the written word's defect removed. The *Phaedrus* did not foresee the second case. Shanahan's regenerate button (§5) is a direct test of 275d.
- **The fabricated assistant.** Socrates on the orator who knows "the differences of human souls" and "when he should speak and when he should refrain" (271c–272b): a specification for a system that adapts its speech to its hearer. Rhetoric "is like medicine" and must know the soul it addresses (270b). The maxim "please … his good and noble masters" not "his fellow-servants" (273e) is an alignment claim.
- **Imitation and commonplaces.** Socrates on Lysias: "the only merit is in the arrangement of them, for there can be none in the invention" (235e–236a); "I have been filled through the ears, like a pitcher, from the waters of another" (235c–d); the Midas epitaph where line order makes no difference (264d). Jowett's "compilations … extracts … imitations" as the mark of a literature's decline. A theory of generation from commonplaces, with and without arrangement.
- **Made beings.** None. The *Phaedrus* has no automaton; its artificial thing is the written speech itself, the "brat" of which Lysias "is the father" (257b), the orphan that "cannot protect or defend themselves" (275e).
- **The mask.** Socrates veils his face to deliver the first speech (237a) and speaks the palinode as "Stesichorus the son of Godly Man … from the town of Desire" (244a). The dialogue is itself a performance of assumed voices.

## 5. Tensions with other readings

- **With Grice.** See the Grice notes, §5. Socrates's written word that "always gives one unvarying answer" is a speaker with whom Grice's hearer cannot do business; but Grice's testimonial writer (p. 52) implicates through a letter.
- **With the *Analects*** (not re-read). Both are records of a teacher's speech made by a writer; the *Phaedrus* is a written argument against writing, and Jowett notices the irony ("in which he may seem also to be doing an injustice to himself").
- **With Wilde, "The Critic as Artist"** (not re-read). A dialogue about whether criticism is creation; the *Phaedrus* on Lysias is criticism of a text's arrangement; Jowett's "Is not all literature passing into criticism" is the same anxiety.
- **With Bergson** (not re-read). The Midas epitaph (264d) as the mechanical: interchangeable parts. Theuth's "wit" (274e) and Thamus's "show of wisdom" (275b).

## 7. Questions the text leaves open

1. Is the chatbot the written word's defect perfected (replies to anyone, never silent, no parent) or repaired (varies its answer, adapts to the hearer, "knows the soul")?
2. Thamus says the inventor "is not always the best judge" of his invention (274e). Who is, for this one?
3. Socrates's living word has "a seed which others brought up in different soils render immortal" (276e–277a). Is a model trained on all written words such a soil, or the garden of Adonis?
4. "The only merit is in the arrangement … there can be none in the invention" (235e–236a): is this a theory of authorship that a model satisfies, and does Socrates mean it?
5. The dialogue walks back into the city at the end. What does the setting do to the argument, and does an argument about writing need a place?
