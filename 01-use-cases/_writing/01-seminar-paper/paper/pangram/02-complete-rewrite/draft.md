# Writing and Dialogue Agents: Plato, Shanahan, and Grice

At *Phaedrus* 275d, Socrates objects that a written speech cannot explain what it says. In Jowett's translation, a reader who questions the speech receives “one unvarying answer.” A language-model dialogue agent can supply a different answer, so it appears to overcome the defect Socrates identifies. That conclusion depends, however, on what counts as answering the original question. An explanation may clarify a claim while preserving it. A correction may replace the claim and specify the evidence for the replacement. A further response may also change the subject without acknowledging that anything has changed. Socrates's objection concerns the first two possibilities. The mere production of another sentence does not distinguish them from the third.

This paper argues that the *Phaedrus* offers a useful account of the demands placed on an answer, but cannot by itself establish that a dialogue agent either satisfies or necessarily fails those demands. The relevant comparison requires examining the procedure that connects one answer to another. Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, and Laria Reynolds provide such an examination in their account of a dialogue agent playing twenty questions. Their example shows why consistency with previous replies does not establish that the agent selected an object before the game began. Their proposed remedy, recording a particular object, also shows that the failure depends on the arrangement of the task. Grice's account of conversational inference explains why a user may nevertheless assume that an earlier selection occurred. Together, these texts support a restricted claim: the apparent continuity of a speaker must be distinguished from the evidence that a conversation has preserved a particular commitment.

The restriction matters because Socrates's criticism of writing makes several demands at once. A written speech cannot explain itself, identify an appropriate reader, or defend itself when challenged (275d–e). A system might improve on writing in one respect while leaving another problem unresolved. The twenty-questions example concerns preservation of a choice, rather than the truth of an explanation or the judgment required to teach a particular learner. Recording the choice addresses the first problem. It does not settle the others. The argument below therefore keeps the separate demands in view, reconstructs what the game establishes, and then considers how Grice helps explain the inference from a responsive exchange to a continuing speaker.

Socrates introduces writing through the disagreement between Theuth and Thamus. Theuth claims that letters will improve the Egyptians' wisdom and memory. Thamus replies that people who rely on written signs will neglect the exercise of memory within themselves (274e–275a). The disagreement is about what the invention does to its users. Theuth treats the availability of a record as a means of acquiring an intellectual capacity. Thamus distinguishes the capacity from the record that assists its exercise. His distinction permits a limited use for writing while rejecting the stronger claim made on its behalf. A person can consult a reminder without thereby acquiring the knowledge needed to explain the matter recorded.

Jowett renders Thamus's distinction as “an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence”; Fowler gives “an elixir not of memory, but of reminding” (275a). The two translations preserve the same correction of the inventor's claim. Fowler's “reminding” makes the dependence especially explicit: a reminder directs attention back to something, but does not specify what understanding the recipient brings to it. Jowett's “reminiscence” can sound more like a capacity possessed by the recipient. Neither version, however, makes the record sufficient for wisdom. The distinction will matter when the role-play paper proposes a written record as a remedy. Such a record may preserve a choice without acquiring every property that Thamus attributes to knowledge.

Thamus next describes pupils who encounter many things without adequate instruction and therefore appear knowledgeable (275a–b). It would be a mistake to equate this description directly with language-model training. Shanahan and his colleagues discuss learning from training data and subsequent adaptation within a supplied context (§§1–2). Those processes require their own explanation. Thamus's claim concerns the relation between reading and instruction within the story Socrates tells. Its relevance to a dialogue agent lies in the question it raises about a particular performance: what additional capacity follows from having words available? Treating that question as already answered by the resemblance between a reader and a trained model would assume the conclusion the comparison is supposed to establish.

The criticism at 275d makes the dependence of writing on an interpreter more specific. Socrates compares a written speech to a painted figure. Both have an appearance that encourages a response appropriate to something living. Questioning the figure produces silence; questioning the speech produces the same words again. The analogy concerns the discrepancy between the expectation created by the representation and the response it can provide. It does not imply that the represented content is false. A painting may resemble its subject, and a proposition may be correct. The difficulty appears when a recipient asks for something further, such as an explanation of how the proposition should be understood.

The translations identify the apparent respondent differently. Jowett writes that “the speaker always gives one unvarying answer,” whereas Fowler says that the written words “always say only one and the same thing” (275d). Jowett's “speaker” makes the reader's attribution of a respondent explicit. Fowler keeps the grammatical subject with the words. An argument that takes Jowett's personification as proof of a speaker behind every text would therefore rely on a feature of the English translation. What both versions establish is more limited: the written expression remains fixed when the reader asks it to do additional explanatory work. A dialogue agent alters that condition, because its next expression can depend on the question. Whether the alteration supplies an explanation still has to be established from what the reply says.

At 275e, Socrates turns from repetition to circulation. Written words reach people who understand them and people for whom they are unsuitable. The speech cannot determine which reader it has encountered. Earlier, Socrates has made such discrimination a requirement of rhetoric: the speaker must learn the kinds of souls and speeches, recognize the person present, and judge the occasion for a particular form of address (271d–272b). These requirements distinguish knowledge of a classification from its application. The rhetorician must connect a general account of an audience with the present listener. In the writing passage, circulation exposes the absence of that judgment at the point of reception.

This criticism allows a precise concession to dialogue agents. A reply can be conditioned on a reader's question, earlier statements, or request for another example. Such conditioning provides a means of adapting the text that a fixed page lacks. It remains possible for the adaptation to be inappropriate. A shorter explanation may remove the distinction the reader needs, or an apparent agreement may be treated as evidence of understanding. The *Phaedrus* does not provide a procedure for deciding whether an agent has judged these cases correctly. It specifies the achievement that would need to be demonstrated: the form of address should suit the learner and the matter being taught. Responsiveness supplies an opportunity to meet that demand, rather than evidence that it has already been met.

The third criticism concerns defense. A written speech that is attacked needs its parent to assist it (275e). Socrates contrasts it with “an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself” (276a). The phrase “of the learner” is essential. The successful teaching of an argument produces someone other than its first speaker who can answer an objection. The relevant continuity therefore cannot consist solely in returning to the original author. The learner must be able to retain the argument and use its grounds in a new encounter. Parentage describes a capacity to support the discourse when challenged, as well as a relation to its origin.

The expression “word graven” also complicates a simple opposition between speech and inscription. Jowett describes the superior word through an act of engraving; Fowler calls it a word “written with intelligence in the mind of the learner” (276a). Both translations preserve an inscription within the account of knowledge that external writing is said to lack. The decisive distinction is consequently the kind of possession and use involved. A learner who can explain and defend a claim does more than retain its wording. A dialogue agent with a stored sentence must likewise do more than reproduce the sentence if its response is to count as a defense. The comparison at this point yields a requirement concerning the use of retained words, not a verdict based on retention alone.

Shanahan and his colleagues describe a dialogue agent as an arrangement in which a model produces text within a turn-taking system and a prompt establishes the initial conversational setting (§2). Their account separates the base model from the way it is used in dialogue. It also states that the paper concentrates on the base model before further reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. This scope limits what can be inferred from its examples about other systems. The account is nevertheless relevant to Socrates's question because it describes how a sequence of written replies comes to be interpreted as the conduct of a speaker. The prompt supplies a role, and the conversation provides further material from which that role develops.

The authors write that an initial characterization can be “extended and/or overwritten” as the conversation proceeds (§3). The alternatives should be kept distinct. Extension can add detail compatible with what has already been said. Overwriting can change the characterization through which later statements are interpreted. A coherent-sounding continuation therefore need not preserve every property attributed to the earlier speaker. This is the point at which the role-play account bears on the paper's claim. The user may understand a reply as an expression of a continuing commitment, while the process described by the authors leaves several compatible continuations available. The particular commitment must be established by something more specific than the fluency of the exchange.

Section 5 specifies that problem through twenty questions. One player chooses an object; the other tries to identify it through questions answered with yes or no. In the proposed interaction, a user asks a dialogue agent to think of an object without stating it. The agent then produces answers compatible with the preceding conversation. The authors describe the remaining possibilities as a set that narrows with each answer. A single final object can be compatible with all those answers even if that object was not selected at the beginning. The sequence may therefore satisfy a condition of consistency while failing to satisfy the temporal condition built into the ordinary rules of the game.

The authors identify a way to expose the difference. After the agent names the object, the user regenerates that final response. The agent will “sometimes name an entirely different object,” one likewise compatible with its previous answers (§5). The word “sometimes” gives the observation its proper scope. The reported possibility of different compatible answers challenges the assumption of one fixed prior selection. It does not establish that every regenerated response changes, or that all dialogue agents behave alike under every arrangement of the task. The evidence concerns a particular inference about what the preceding transcript records. The final answer is insufficient to identify a choice supposedly made before the transcript's questioning began.

Two rules for evaluating the game now become distinguishable. Under the ordinary rule, the final answer must disclose the object selected before the questions. Under a weaker rule, it need only name an object compatible with the answers already given. A transcript can satisfy the weaker rule while supplying no evidence that it satisfies the stronger one. The distinction explains why another fluent reply would not resolve the problem. If the agent is asked whether it really selected the object earlier, its assurance would still be a later statement. What is missing is a constraint established at the relevant earlier point. The problem is thus an identifiable absence in the procedure, rather than a general objection to generated language.

Footnote 4 directly addresses the absence. It says that the agent could record a particular object in a hidden monologue or in an encoded form within the visible dialogue. The proposed change supplies an earlier record against which the later answer can be compared. It does not make the game successful merely by adding more text. The record must specify one object before the questions, remain available, and constrain or be checked against the final disclosure. If the final answer names a different object, the violation becomes identifiable. The repair supplies evidence relevant to the stronger rule because the object no longer has to be inferred exclusively from the final answer.

The footnote also limits the analogy between the secret object and the agent's role. In the main text, the authors use the absence of commitment to one object to explain the absence of commitment to one completely specified role (§5). Their remedy shows that an object can be fixed through an added operation. It therefore prevents the game from proving that all commitments are necessarily unavailable to the system. This does not refute the role-play account. A recorded object and a fully specified character are different things. It does mean that conclusions about commitment require specifying which commitment is at issue and what the surrounding procedure records. The example is strongest as an analysis of that procedure; it becomes less conclusive when generalized to every possible property of the apparent speaker.

The authors themselves distinguish their explanatory vocabulary from a claim that possible roles must be explicitly represented inside the model (§5). This qualification matters for the interpretation of the game. Their term “superposition” describes the range of continuations compatible with the exchange; it need not designate a literal list stored in a particular place. The evidence from regeneration concerns variation in the outputs under the described conditions. The recorded-choice remedy, in contrast, introduces an explicit representation with an identifiable content. Moving between these descriptions without marking the difference would confuse an account of possible behavior with an account of the records used to constrain it. The paper's comparison requires the latter when assessing whether a particular choice persists.

The remedy returns the discussion to writing. A fixed inscription supplies something that the variable sequence of replies did not establish on its own. In this restricted respect, the property Socrates criticizes at 275d becomes useful: the record says the same thing when consulted later. The usefulness depends on the question being asked. A record of the chosen object need not explain why the object was chosen; it needs to identify which object was chosen. If the subsequent inquiry asks for an explanation of a philosophical claim, repetition will again be insufficient. The apparent reversal of Socrates's objection therefore depends on a change of task. The same stability can satisfy a demand for identification and fail a demand for explanation.

Grice helps explain why the user may initially overlook this distinction. His Cooperative Principle concerns the contribution required by the accepted purpose or direction of an exchange at a given stage (45). Participants interpret individual remarks in relation to that purpose. They do not normally treat each sentence as an isolated item whose relevance must be established from nothing. An invitation to play twenty questions supplies a familiar activity with expectations about the order of its operations. The request to think of an object is understood as initiating the selection on which the rest of the activity depends. Subsequent yes-or-no answers can reinforce that understanding without independently confirming that the expected selection took place.

Grice's garage example shows the structure of such an inference. One person says, “I am out of petrol”; another replies, “There is a garage round the corner” (51). The second sentence does not explicitly say that the garage is open or sells petrol. Those possibilities enter the interpretation because the reply is presumed relevant to the first person's problem. The hearer reasons from the accepted purpose of the exchange to something the speaker must at least regard as possible. The content inferred exceeds the proposition literally stated, but the excess has an identifiable basis in the situation. If the garage were known to be closed, the hearer would need another explanation of why it was mentioned.

The twenty-questions interaction invites a related inference. Answers about an object's properties appear relevant because they are understood as information about the object previously selected. The rules of the activity supply the unstated connection, just as the need for petrol supplies the connection in Grice's example. The difference emerges when the agent can generate compatible answers without having performed the initial selection. In that case, the user has inferred a condition that the visible replies do not establish. The replies may remain individually consistent while the expected history of the interaction is missing. The regeneration procedure matters because it gives the user a reason to reconsider that inferred history.

This application of Grice does not require treating every attribution to an agent as a mistake. His account specifies the assumptions through which an inference can be worked out. The evidential question is whether those assumptions hold in the case under consideration. A recorded selection would give the user an additional basis for assuming that later answers concern one prior object. A transcript without such a record may leave the matter unresolved. The value of the Gricean account is its ability to locate the point at which the interpretation depends on background expectations. It then becomes possible to investigate that point, rather than simply accepting or rejecting the apparent speaker as a whole.

Grice's testimonial example makes the dependence on background information more demanding. A teacher writes a recommendation for a philosophy candidate that praises the candidate's English and regular attendance, while saying nothing about philosophical ability (52). Grice explains the implied negative judgment by eliminating alternatives. The writer has undertaken to recommend the candidate, knows the candidate as a pupil, and knows what information is wanted. The omission consequently requires explanation. The inference is not licensed by the words of praise alone. It depends on the relation between the letter's purpose, the writer's knowledge, and the information withheld. Without those premises, the same wording would provide a weaker basis for the negative judgment.

That example clarifies what is insufficient about asking whether a dialogue agent sounds like someone with reasons. A generated recommendation could reproduce the wording of Grice's example without standing in the relation to the candidate that his explanation assumes. The reader would then need evidence about the information supplied to the system and the instructions governing the task before drawing the same inference from omission. A system supplied with a dossier and asked to assess a candidate would present a different case from one merely asked to continue an example letter. The wording can remain similar while the available grounds for interpretation change. The distinction concerns the conditions of the particular communicative act, not a general division between meaningful and meaningless prose.

Grice's account of Quality introduces a related distinction between an answer and its grounds. His maxims include “Do not say what you believe to be false” and “Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence” (46). The second maxim is especially useful for the present comparison because an answer can be assessed in relation to evidence made available within an exchange. The presence of a citation alone does not establish that relation. The cited passage must support the proposition for which it is offered. If the passage conflicts with the proposition, a subsequent reply should either justify the reading or revise the proposition. This is one identifiable way in which a responsive exchange can do the work that Socrates finds unavailable in the isolated written speech.

Grice does not treat the demand for information as independent of the demand for evidence. In his example of a planned journey, a speaker gives only the approximate location of a mutual acquaintance, although the questioner needs something more precise (51–52). The limited reply can be explained by a conflict between providing sufficient information and having sufficient evidence. The interpretation depends on understanding why the answer stops where it does. This example gives a clearer comparison with Socrates's requirement to know when to speak or remain silent than the simple presence or absence of another turn. A request for more information, a qualified answer, or a refusal to specify a town can each be examined for its relation to the available evidence and the purpose of the exchange.

The relevant test of a philosophical reply can now be stated concretely. Suppose an interpretation of the *Phaedrus* asserts that Socrates denies writing any useful role. The discussion of reminders at 276d supplies a passage against that assertion. A further reply that merely repeats the assertion fails to address the evidence. A reply that silently substitutes a more qualified position loses the relation between the earlier claim and its correction. A reply that distinguishes writing as a reminder from writing as sufficient instruction identifies both the error and the reason for revising it. This is a hypothetical example, but the textual conflict on which it depends is available for inspection. The improvement consists in an altered interpretation supported by a particular passage.

Such a correction would establish a limited achievement. It would show that the exchange has connected an objection to a revision. It would not by itself prove that the same procedure succeeds on other passages, or that the system possesses everything Socrates means by knowledge. Those further claims require further evidence. This limitation follows from the form of the argument rather than from a presumption about the agent. The twenty-questions repair is similarly limited: preserving the selected object establishes one condition of that game. Treating either success as proof of a general intellectual capacity would repeat Theuth's error of ascribing a broader benefit to an invention than the described operation establishes.

A stronger objection to this account is that a later justification may be invented after the answer it purports to explain. The role-play analysis gives reason to distinguish an account of how an earlier reply was produced from a reason for accepting what it says. A model could generate a sound textual objection after having produced the disputed interpretation without considering it. In that event, the objection's force would depend on its relation to the text, while a claim that it reports the model's earlier reasoning would need separate support. The distinction is required even within ordinary argument: discovering a reason later can justify a revision without showing that the reason caused the original position. What matters for the revised interpretation is whether the stated inference holds.

Grice's requirement that a conversational implicature be capable of being worked out makes this distinction useful rather than merely terminological (50). His explanations reconstruct the assumptions from which the interpretation follows. They are not presented as transcripts of every thought that occurred in the hearer's mind. Applying a comparable standard to the revised reading of the *Phaedrus* means making its inference reconstructible: the earlier assertion excluded every use for writing; the reminder passage permits a use; the assertion therefore needs qualification. A later reader can assess that inference without accepting an unsupported account of the producing system's internal history. The reconstruction supplies grounds for the reading, while leaving claims about the production process to an appropriate source of evidence.

The main objection from Plato remains. The record proposed in footnote 4 exists outside the learner, whereas Socrates locates the defensible word in the learner's soul. The successful correction of one interpretation also falls short of the developed rhetorical judgment described at 271d–272b. These are substantial differences between Plato's account and the procedures examined here. They prevent the recorded-choice remedy from being presented as a full satisfaction of his ideal. They do not, however, remove the difference between an exchange that preserves the chosen object and one that merely produces a compatible object at the end. The remedy answers the narrower question posed by the game, even if the larger philosophical account requires more.

The distinction between a narrow result and a complete account also appears within the *Phaedrus*. At 275b–c, Phaedrus questions Socrates's ability to invent stories about Egypt. Socrates replies that earlier hearers would accept truth even from “oak or rock,” whereas Phaedrus attends to the speaker's identity and origin. The remark does not abolish the subsequent demand that a speech be defensible. It distinguishes the source's credentials from the truth of what is said. An invented origin cannot establish a claim, but criticism of its origin does not yet refute it. The reader still has to examine the proposition. This sequence makes it difficult to interpret the writing passage as a rule that the absence of a particular kind of speaker decides every question about the resulting words.

The exchange also changes the burden of argument. Phaedrus's objection identifies a possible fiction in the story's presentation. Socrates requires attention to whether the account of writing is correct. The two questions can be separated without making provenance irrelevant. If the story were offered as historical evidence, its invention would directly affect the claim it supports. If it presents an argument about the uses of writing, that argument requires examination on its stated grounds. The same distinction applies to a dialogue agent's reply. A claim about a recorded earlier choice requires evidence of that record. An interpretation of a passage requires evidence from the passage. A generated assurance about either matter cannot perform both evidential functions merely by sounding authoritative.

Socrates's account of his own borrowed speech further complicates the demand for an identifiable origin. Before his first speech on love, he says that he has been filled through the ears from another's words and has forgotten who supplied them (235c–d). The speech is nevertheless available for examination. His uncertainty about its source does not make its propositions disappear or decide whether they are defensible. Later, the phrase “the father of the brat” assigns Lysias a relation to the speech Socrates has delivered (257b). The dialogue thus distinguishes the present speaker, the sources of a speech, and the person associated with its position. A claim's origin and its current defense may involve different relations. Returning to a single speaker is not the only way the dialogue organizes argumentative responsibility.

Plato's written composition provides a further case in which those relations are distributed. The reader receives both Socrates's questions and Phaedrus's responses through a fixed text. The apparent success of a question within the scene cannot by itself establish that the argument will persuade another reader. That reader must reconstruct the claims and assess the responses. The dialogue can support this work by preserving the objection and its answer together. It cannot complete every future act of interpretation in advance. This account assigns a definite use to the written dialogue without claiming that its existence refutes Socrates. Its stability makes the exchange available for examination; the examination remains an additional operation performed on it.

The discussion of reminders at 276d expressly permits such continued use of written words. Socrates allows a knowledgeable person to preserve them for a later period of forgetfulness and for others following the same course of inquiry. The record's value depends on a relation to subsequent activity. This is the point of contact with the recorded object in twenty questions: writing preserves something that a later operation can consult. The difference is equally important. Identifying an object from its record is a narrower task than defending a philosophical position. The comparison establishes that external records can perform a necessary role in an inquiry. It leaves the adequacy of the subsequent operation to be assessed on its own terms.

At 264c, Socrates requires a discourse to have parts adapted to each other and to the whole. This demand applies to the extended sequence through which an interpretation is defended. A local correction may require a change in the conclusion if the earlier claim supported it. An exchange that acknowledges the reminder passage but continues to conclude that writing has no useful role would remain inconsistent. Keeping the earlier assertion and its grounds available makes that inconsistency easier to identify. The useful record must therefore preserve more than isolated answers. It must allow a reader to determine which claims depend on the corrected one. This requirement explains why saving a transcript is helpful without being sufficient for a coherent argument.

The concluding comparison with cultivation supplies a related criterion. Socrates contrasts the brief display of a garden of Adonis with the work of planting that produces continuing growth (276b–277a). His account of dialectic ends with words that produce further words in other minds. The point relevant here is the continuation of the inquiry beyond its initial presentation. A learner who can identify why an interpretation was corrected can apply the distinction to another passage. A stored answer that is repeated without that use would satisfy only the narrower condition of retention. The image therefore supports the distinction maintained throughout this paper between keeping a claim available and exercising the capacities needed to defend or revise it.

The comparison yields a limited but determinate conclusion. A dialogue agent overcomes the literal repetition of a written page by producing another response, but this change does not establish all the capacities Socrates requires. The twenty-questions example identifies one missing condition: a prior choice must be preserved if the final answer is to disclose that choice. Its footnote supplies a way to meet that condition through a record. Grice explains why the user may otherwise infer the choice from the apparent cooperation of the replies. The appropriate object of assessment is therefore the connection established among a claim, the record or evidence relevant to it, and the subsequent answer. In the *Phaedrus*, the difference between a reminder and a defensible account remains necessary even when the words can answer back.
