In the Phaedrus, Socrates compares writing to painting. The painted figures appear alive, yet questions meet with silence. Written speeches produce a related frustration: "if you want to know anything and put a question to one of them, the speaker always gives one unvarying answer" (275d). A dialogue agent seems to remove precisely this defect. It answers a follow-up, alters an explanation, supplies an objection, and reformulates a claim for a different reader. The page has acquired the behavior Socrates wanted from its absent speaker. Yet the ability to produce a second answer leaves another question open: what connects that answer to the first? A reply can explain an earlier claim, abandon it for a reason, or replace it without acknowledging the change. All three are responsive. Their differences matter to anyone trying to learn through conversation.

Reading the Phaedrus alongside Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, and Laria Reynolds's "Role-Play with Large Language Models" makes that distinction unusually precise. The ancient dialogue asks what enables words to survive questioning; the modern paper describes a system that can continue many possible conversations. Their conjunction suggests that answerability depends on relations among claims, evidence, interlocutors, and subsequent revisions. A stable human personality cannot serve as the sole test of those relations: Plato stages borrowed and invented speeches, and his own written dialogue continues to generate inquiry without his presence. Nor does a stream of responsive language establish them automatically. The central problem is how a conversation preserves enough of its past to make a change intelligible. Dialogue agents make this problem newly conspicuous, while also supplying tools through which it can be addressed.

The phrase "one unvarying answer" is therefore a starting point rather than a complete diagnosis. Variation can improve an explanation, but constancy can preserve a commitment. The reader of a book can return to a sentence and show exactly where an interpretation went wrong. A conversational system that readily produces a different sentence might make that comparison harder unless its earlier answer remains available. To assess the difference, we need to separate the continuity of a text, the continuity of a speaker, and the continuity of an inquiry. H. P. Grice's account of conversational cooperation provides a third term between Plato's living teacher and Shanahan and his colleagues' enacted roles. It helps explain how participants treat successive utterances as contributions to a shared undertaking, and where that treatment can exceed what an exchange actually establishes.

Socrates introduces the problem of writing through a story about the Egyptian inventor Theuth and the king Thamus. Theuth presents letters as a technology that will improve both wisdom and memory. In Jowett's translation, they are "a specific both for the memory and for the wit" (274e). The promise joins a means of storing words to an intellectual transformation in their users. Thamus separates the two. The inventor, attached to his invention, has mistaken what it makes available for what its users acquire. Written signs will encourage people to recall things from outside themselves; they will encounter many statements without undergoing the instruction that makes those statements knowledge. The disagreement concerns a causal claim about learning, not simply the availability of information.

The competing translations sharpen this distinction. Where Jowett has "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence," Fowler gives "an elixir not of memory, but of reminding" (275a). In both versions, the same device receives a revised description. Its usefulness survives, but its operation has been specified differently. A reminder can be effective precisely because someone already knows what to do with it. A remembered sentence, by contrast, can be available without its reader being able to explain its implications. Thamus is asking what happens between access and understanding. His answer rests on a strong preference for internal possession, but his question remains useful even where that preference is disputed: what capacities does this arrangement produce, and where do those capacities reside?

The modern comparison becomes weak if it simply equates a model's training with the Egyptians' reading without instruction (275a–b). Shanahan and his colleagues describe training, prompting, and the capacity to continue a pattern supplied in context. These are substantive operations, even if they differ from Plato's account of teaching. The relevant issue is how a particular operation supports a particular achievement. A system may retrieve a passage, connect it to another, or articulate an objection; those accomplishments require separate assessment. Equally, a reader can memorize a text while remaining unable to use it. Thamus's contrast exposes a gap between possession and exercise, but it does not settle in advance whether an external arrangement can help close that gap.

Theuth's presentation also places the argument inside a contest over descriptions of a tool. The inventor announces its benefit; the king redescribes its effects. Neither description is simply the object itself. This matters because claims about dialogue agents often move just as quickly from a visible performance to a general account of what the performance means. A fluent explanation can be taken as evidence of understanding, while a failure can be taken as proof that every success was empty. The Egyptian story encourages a more discriminating question about mechanisms and consequences. What does a specific use make possible? What does it leave unpracticed? Which features of the surrounding activity could change the answer? These questions concern the design of inquiry as much as the character of the instrument.

After the story, Socrates develops three connected criticisms of written discourse. The first concerns interrogation: the words remain the same when questioned. The second concerns circulation: a written speech passes among readers who understand it and readers for whom it is unsuitable. The third concerns defense: when mishandled or attacked, it requires its parent to help it (275d–e). These criticisms share a concern with the relation between an utterance and its circumstances, but they describe different failures. An explanation may be fixed yet well suited to a reader. A responsive speaker may misunderstand an audience. A circulating argument may be defensible even when its author is unavailable. Keeping the three charges distinct prevents conversational adaptability from being mistaken for a complete solution or dismissed as irrelevant.

The first charge turns on a tension within repetition. When a reader asks a written proposition what it means, the marks do not change. Yet their sameness also allows the reader to identify what needs explanation. A second passage, a commentary, or another reader can then help. Socrates concentrates on the text's inability to undertake that work itself. His complaint is strongest when a reader expects the written speech to function as a teacher on its own. It is weaker as an account of a reading practice that already includes comparison and discussion. Indeed, the dialogue before us is one of the things readers can compare and discuss. Its unchanging sentences have become the basis for inquiries that neither Socrates nor Plato is present to supervise.

The second charge concerns a speech's inability to select or recognize its audience. The surrounding discussion of rhetoric gives this concern a specific shape. An accomplished speaker must understand kinds of souls and kinds of discourse, then recognize the person encountered in practice. A general classification must become a situated judgment. The speaker must also grasp occasions for speaking, keeping silent, brevity, and intensity (271d–272b). This account asks more than whether a reply mentions something the listener previously said. It asks whether a form of address is appropriate to an intellectual and emotional situation. Nevertheless, a system that changes its explanation in response to a reader has acquired a capacity the isolated written page lacks. Plato's categories allow us to acknowledge that change while asking how its adequacy could be tested.

The third charge, dependence on a parent, makes the strongest demand. A speech should be able to withstand an objection through the knowledge from which it proceeds. Socrates presents its superior sibling as "an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself, and knows when to speak and when to be silent" (276a). The location is significant: the word lives in the learner, not merely in the original author's custody. Teaching has succeeded when another person can carry the argument forward. Parentage thus names a relation of support and reproduction as well as an origin. An author's identity alone cannot fulfill it. The author must be able to do something for the claim when its grounds are challenged.

Jowett's introduction supplies a useful resistance to the hierarchy these passages establish. He describes speech as "more transitory, more diffuse, more elastic and capable of adaptation to moods and times", while writing is "more permanent, more concentrated," addressed "to all the world." The qualities Socrates treats as weaknesses can also be conditions of intellectual reach. Writing travels because it is released from a particular encounter. Its permanence enables a reader to inspect an argument at a different pace and return with a different question. A dialogue agent joins features from both sides of Jowett's distinction. It can adapt a response to an immediate interlocutor while producing text that can be stored, circulated, and reread. That combination complicates any simple choice between living conversation and dead inscription.

Shanahan and his colleagues begin their account of dialogue agents with an equally consequential distinction between components. A language model is placed in a turn-taking arrangement that alternates generated text with user contributions. A dialogue prompt establishes a scene, much like the opening of a script, and subsequent exchanges develop it (§2). Conversation is already an arrangement of model, interface, instructions, and accumulated text. The agent encountered by a user cannot be described adequately by pointing only to a stream of predictions or only to the personality implied by its replies. Each description isolates something useful, but the interaction depends on their organization. The question of answerability must therefore concern the arrangement through which an answer is produced and retained.

The authors' role-play account explains why the apparent speaker can be both coherent and flexible. The prompt initially sketches a character, but the conversation extends or overwrites that sketch (§3). Their improvisational analogy emphasizes that the model need not select one fully specified character in advance and then faithfully express that character's settled interior. Multiple continuations remain possible, and the sampled conversation develops one path through them (§4). This description makes the user an active contributor to the emerging role. A request for severity, sympathy, or expertise may change how the apparent speaker answers. It also means that a later explanation of an earlier statement is itself another contribution to the developing performance. Its explanatory value has to be established through its relation to the statement and its evidence.

The twenty-questions example exposes this relation particularly well. The authors describe prompting an agent to think of an object without saying what it is, then answering questions about it (§5). A human player would ordinarily select an object and let that selection constrain subsequent answers. In the described setup, compatible possibilities can remain open as the dialogue proceeds. When asked eventually to reveal the object, the agent may generate one consistent with the previous answers. Regenerating that final response can sometimes yield a different compatible object. The transcript can therefore exhibit consistency without preserving a single earlier selection. Those are different achievements, and the form of the game encourages the player to infer the second from the first.

The example is especially useful because it distinguishes two meanings of answering a question correctly. If the rule is to name any object compatible with the recorded answers, either final object may succeed. If the rule is to reveal the object selected at the beginning, compatibility alone is insufficient. The visible exchange underdetermines which game has been played. The difficulty arises from an unstated expectation about continuity: the user assumes that an earlier act of choosing took place and remained binding. This is a more specific problem than the claim that generated language lacks meaning or that dialogue agents cannot reason. It identifies a missing commitment within a particular procedure, one that can be investigated by changing the procedure.

The authors make precisely that move in footnote 4. They say the shortcoming is easily overcome by having the agent record a particular object, either in a hidden monologue or in an encoded form within the visible dialogue. This qualification is decisive. A conclusion that the agent can never maintain a commitment would contradict the account being cited. The proposed repair gives an earlier event a persistent representation against which later answers can be assessed. The repair does not by itself establish that every answer will follow the record, but it makes a violation identifiable. It changes the activity from maintaining compatibility with an expanding conversation to honoring an inspectable prior choice. Memory here is a property of the organized interaction, supported through writing.

That last feature reverses the terms of Thamus's objection in a productive way. An external inscription, supposedly the rival of inward memory, can become the means by which a conversational agent acquires continuity. The comparison does not erase Plato's distinction between a reminder and knowledge. It shows why the location of a record cannot settle the value of its use. A written commitment can constrain a later response; a page of notes can make an inconsistency available for examination. What matters next is whether the conversation actually returns to that record and what it does with a conflict. The technical remedy produces a philosophical question about the relation between preserved words and the operations performed upon them.

The title's "unvarying answer" consequently has two functions. It names the obstacle that prevents a static text from explaining itself, and it names the stability that lets an interlocutor identify what requires explanation. A productive dialogue needs both persistence and transformation. An explanation should be able to change its wording while retaining the claim it explains; a revision should be able to change the claim while identifying why. Without persistence, every challenge can be absorbed into a fresh performance. Without transformation, a challenge receives only repetition. The distinction between these failures cannot be captured by counting how many different responses a system produces. It requires comparing the reasons and commitments that connect the responses.

Continuity also needs to be distinguished from stubbornness. A speaker who refuses to revise an exposed contradiction has maintained a position while failing to answer for it. Socrates's demand for defense cannot mean that the same conclusion must survive every encounter unchanged, since examination can discover that the conclusion was mistaken. The relevant stability belongs to the undertaking: participants remain engaged with the question and its evidence even when their judgment changes. A system designed merely to repeat a stored answer would therefore reproduce the original defect of writing. The recorded commitment becomes useful when it can be brought into a relation with a new objection. It establishes what has changed and enables the change to count as learning within the inquiry, rather than as the unexplained replacement of one fluent performance by another.

Consider a hypothetical exchange about the writing passage itself. An agent initially says that Socrates rejects all uses of writing. A reader then supplies the discussion of written reminders at 276d. Several continuations become possible. The agent might repeat the original claim, silently replace it with a more qualified one, or explicitly distinguish the rejection of writing as sufficient instruction from its permitted use as a reminder. The third response makes an argumentative improvement that can be checked against the passage. Its value is available in the changed distinction and the supporting text. Whether the response expresses a stable character behind the dialogue is a further question; the local improvement does not have to wait for that question to be resolved.

This example also reveals why a display of self-criticism is insufficient on its own. An answer can announce that a previous answer was mistaken while supplying no better account of the evidence. Conversely, a concise correction may demonstrate its grounds without a dramatic confession. Answerability consists in making the relevant relation visible: this earlier claim conflicts with this passage; this revised claim accommodates it; these further consequences follow. A transcript preserves the steps, while source references make them examinable outside the conversation. Such a procedure gives the learner more than a new conclusion. It provides a reusable distinction and an example of how textual evidence can force an argument to change.

The role-play paper itself supports taking these consequences seriously. The authors distinguish the simulator from the roles it simulates and observe that enacted behavior can have real effects (§6). Their framework is intended to explain those effects without treating every apparent intention as evidence of a separate, enduring character inside the model. In the section on truthfulness, they also discuss how a helpful role can produce a correct answer to an ordinary factual question (§7). Role-play is therefore not a synonym for universal falsehood. It describes a way of producing behavior whose claims can be true, false, useful, or misleading in particular circumstances. An account of academic dialogue should assess those circumstances rather than treating the framework as a verdict on every resulting sentence.

The same precision improves the treatment of Plato's second charge. A dialogue agent conditions its next answer on what its interlocutor has supplied. It may offer another example, adopt a different vocabulary, or explain an omitted step. These are observable forms of adaptation. Whether they amount to the rhetorical knowledge Socrates demands depends on further relations among the user's difficulty, the explanation, and the progress of the inquiry. An agent might mistake agreement for understanding or produce an easier formulation that loses the argument's central distinction. A teacher might do the same. Plato supplies a demanding account of audience-sensitive instruction; he does not supply a simple test that a change of vocabulary either passes or fails by itself.

Silence requires similar care. Socrates associates skilled speech with knowing when to withhold it, but a refusal, a request for clarification, and an admission that the evidence is insufficient can all perform forms of restraint. The fact that they appear as written turns does not make them conceptually incapable of doing so. Their adequacy depends on the occasion. A request for clarification may preserve an inquiry when an immediate answer would assume the point in dispute. It may also be pointless when the necessary information has already been supplied. The relevant distinction concerns sensitivity to reasons for speaking or pausing. Counting silence as the absence of characters on a screen would confuse a rhetorical achievement with a feature of the interface.

Grice offers a way to describe the expectations through which readers recognize such achievements. His Cooperative Principle asks for the contribution required by the accepted purpose or direction of the exchange at its current stage (45). The purpose can develop during conversation; it need not be an elaborate plan agreed in advance. Still, some moves count as suitable and others do not. This is why a coherent sequence differs from a collection of individually plausible sentences. Each contribution bears on an undertaking whose shape depends partly on what has already happened. In a discussion of the Phaedrus, a request to justify a quotation creates a different conversational obligation from a request to invent an alternative ending.

Grice's maxims of Quality distinguish truth-directed contribution from performance alone: "Do not say what you believe to be false" and "Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence" (46). Their wording invokes a speaker's beliefs and evidential position. Applied to a role-playing system, that vocabulary raises interpretive questions about which level of description is appropriate. Yet the demand for evidence also has an operational form. A claim can be accompanied by a passage, a calculation, or a record that another participant can examine. This does not reproduce every feature of Grice's account of human intention. It establishes a public relation that supports some of the work the maxim organizes, including the ability to challenge an assertion for specified reasons.

Grice's account of implicature shows where conversational appearances can lead a reader further. His model explanation includes the recursive formulation "he knows (and knows that I know that he knows)" (50). The hearer infers a meaning partly by assuming the speaker recognizes what the hearer can work out. An apparently purposeful omission or an unexpectedly indirect remark can therefore carry more than its literal content. With a dialogue agent, a reader may make a comparable inference from the shape of a reply: the agent omitted a qualification because it judged the qualification unnecessary, or stressed a phrase because it recognized the reader's confusion. The reply might support that interpretation in practice, but its rhetorical shape alone does not establish the history of judgments the reader attributes to it.

This gap helps explain why the twenty-questions example is persuasive. The initial invitation to think of an object activates a familiar cooperative script. The user supplies an intention and a temporal sequence that the visible answers do not independently verify. When the final object changes, the surprise reveals an inference about the earlier exchange. A recorded selection resolves that particular ambiguity by adding evidence about the procedure. The record is valuable because it makes one conversational assumption checkable. The lesson extends beyond guessing games: a declared research question, a source list, or an explicit definition can supply the persistent object around which a changing discussion is organized. Each gives later contributions something determinate to answer to.

Grice also prevents cooperation from being confused with harmony or a shared personality. His examples of practical assistance allow participants to have different ultimate aims while sharing an immediate task (48). An exchange can likewise organize useful contributions without requiring identical motives or a complete account of the participants' inner lives. In a reading exercise, the local purpose might be to determine whether a quoted sentence supports a proposed interpretation. A reader and an agent can produce a passage comparison, identify a contradiction, and revise the interpretation within that task. The result can be assessed through the work performed. Claims about the broader identity of the apparent speaker remain distinct from this local record of cooperation.

A strong objection remains: publicly available reasons may be generated after the fact. A system asked why it made a claim can supply a plausible justification that was never a cause of its earlier answer. The role-play account gives good grounds for keeping this distinction visible. But explanatory sequence and argumentative validity are themselves different matters. A later justification can provide a valid reason to accept a proposition even if it does not describe the causal history of its production. Conversely, an accurate account of how a sentence was generated can leave its truth entirely unresolved. A close-reading dialogue needs reasons that withstand examination; a study of model behavior may additionally need evidence about the mechanism that produced them. Neither inquiry is served by silently substituting one kind of explanation for the other.

The paper's claim is therefore narrower than a theory of machine consciousness and more demanding than a celebration of interactivity. Answerability, in the sense developed here, concerns the inspectable connections through which a claim can be defended, corrected, or withdrawn. A dialogue system can support those connections, and the role-play framework identifies ways they may be missing. Its success in a given exchange requires evidence from that exchange. This approach leaves open questions about the subjective or metaphysical status of the apparent speaker while making the academic task concrete. Which claim is under examination? What counts against it? What has changed? What can a subsequent reader reconstruct? Those questions apply to the entire arrangement of participants, texts, and tools.

The Phaedrus itself makes a purely personal account of authority difficult to maintain. Socrates repeatedly speaks through forms whose origins he disclaims or complicates. Before his first speech about love, he compares himself to a vessel: "I have been filled through the ears, like a pitcher, from the waters of another," while admitting that he has forgotten his informant (235d). The image locates speech within a history of reception that the present speaker cannot fully recover. His ability to produce an argument exceeds his ability to identify every source from which it comes. This does not exempt the argument from questioning. It shows that the relation between a speaking person and the origin of a speech is already unsettled within the dialogue's own practice.

The language of parentage becomes still more complicated when Socrates calls Lysias "the father of the brat" and asks that there be no more of his progeny (257b). Attribution distributes responsibility across performances rather than merely identifying the mouth from which words emerge. Socrates has delivered a speech shaped by an earlier one, then repudiated its orientation. A word's parent can name an influence, a source of its structure, or someone answerable for the position it advances. These relations can overlap without being identical. To ask who supports an argument when it is challenged is consequently more exact than to assume every speech has one sovereign origin whose presence would resolve all interpretive difficulties.

The Egyptian story makes the problem explicit. Phaedrus remarks that Socrates can invent stories about Egypt or elsewhere. Socrates replies by recalling hearers willing to accept truth even from "oak or rock," then criticizes concern with who speaks and where the story comes from (275b–c). The passage interrupts the very argument that will soon insist on a written speech's need for its parent. A reading that makes personal origin the sole ground of authority struggles to accommodate both moments. Their conjunction suggests a distinction between a claim's credentials and its grounds. A prestigious origin cannot make a false assertion true; an unusual origin cannot make a true one false. Yet a claim may still need assistance when its grounds are disputed.

The distinction also changes how we understand Plato's written form. The dialogue preserves questions, changes of direction, invented voices, and objections within a text that cannot itself respond to a new reader. Its characters model ways of testing a claim, while the arrangement of their speeches invites further testing. Plato does not simply transmit a conclusion about the inadequacy of transmission. He makes a written object whose usefulness partly depends on a reader's reconstruction of the relations among its parts. The existence of that object does not refute Socrates's account by itself. It does, however, make it difficult to treat external inscription as incapable of participating in learning. The dialogue asks to be used in a way that exceeds passive reception.

The dialogue form also creates a difficulty for any easy appeal to Socrates as the model of a fully open encounter. Plato writes the questions as well as the answers. Phaedrus's agreements and objections arrive through the same composed artifact as Socrates's responses. A reader sees an image of successful instruction, with no independent access to a learner outside that image. The text's intellectual force consequently depends on whether its distinctions can be reconstructed and tested beyond the scene it stages. A simulated classroom raises a comparable question. The appearance of several interlocutors can make an argument easier to inspect, but an exchange of agreeable voices establishes little on its own. A valuable objection must identify an actual difficulty in the position, and the response must do more than restore the rhythm of agreement. The test again concerns the relations among the claims, rather than the number or vividness of the speakers.

A dialogue agent can extend this possibility by producing additional questions or alternative readings around a preserved text. It can also obscure the text by endlessly substituting new paraphrases for the passage at issue. Both outcomes are compatible with responsiveness. The difference depends on whether the activity keeps returning to the object of inquiry and whether its transformations leave a usable record. A generated objection tied to a sentence can open an interpretive problem; an invented quotation can close it falsely. A comparison of translations can expose a meaningful lexical choice; a confident synthesis can conceal that choice. These are differences in the relation between operations and evidence, and they can be investigated without deciding the value of all generated discourse in advance.

There is a serious practical objection to this account of distributed answerability. A record can be extensive without being useful. Saving every turn does not guarantee that anyone can identify the disputed claim, reconstruct the relevant source, or distinguish a correction from a restatement. An apparatus of quotations and logs can become another surface of apparent knowledge. Thamus's concern returns at the level of the archive: the presence of reminders does not establish the capacity to use them. The response must therefore concern organization as well as preservation. An effective record connects each consequential claim to its evidence and makes important changes visible. It supports particular acts of examination rather than merely accumulating material that looks like the residue of intellectual labor.

Plato's account of the form of a speech supplies a further criterion. A discourse should resemble a living creature whose parts bear fitting relations to one another and to the whole (264c). A sequence of locally plausible answers may lack that organization. Each reply can satisfy the immediate prompt while quietly changing the larger thesis. The requirement of a connected argument asks more: the conclusion should follow from distinctions maintained across the inquiry, and a revision in one part should have consequences elsewhere. Here a persistent outline or statement of the thesis can serve the same function as the recorded object in twenty questions. It supplies something against which the evolving performance can be compared, while remaining open to explicit revision.

This use of writing brings the argument back to Socrates's gardener. The garden of Adonis produces a beautiful display in eight days, while serious cultivation works toward a different kind of growth (276b). The temporal contrast is easy to turn into a simple equation between speed and superficiality. The subsequent account of dialectic makes the criterion more precise. Its words yield seeds from which further words grow in other minds (276e–277a). The valuable result is generative: an encounter equips someone to continue an inquiry under changed circumstances. A rapidly produced explanation could contribute to that result, and a slowly prepared lecture could fail to do so. Duration describes the image; the ability to carry the inquiry forward gives the image its intellectual force.

On this reading, the relevant test of a conversational exercise comes after the polished answer. Can its distinctions be used on another passage? Can an objection be reconstructed and answered? Can the resulting argument survive a change of audience without losing its grounds? These are capacities that a dialogue, a written text, and a computational arrangement can jointly support. The living word in the learner names Plato's preferred destination, while the modern arrangement makes the route more distributed. A source becomes a note, a note becomes a prompt, a reply becomes an objection, and the objection changes a paper that another reader can question. The circulation of outputs into inputs acquires intellectual value when these transitions preserve and develop the reasons for what is said.

The transfer need not require every participant to perform every operation unaided. The distinction between using a reminder and knowing how to use it already allows differentiated tasks. One component can preserve quotations, another can propose a reading, and another can compare the reading with a difficult passage. What the inquiry needs is a way for those contributions to bear on each other. A corrected citation should alter an interpretation when its wording matters; an objection to the thesis should reach the conclusion. Such coordination gives the archive a function beyond storage and the conversation a function beyond continuation. It also identifies an attainable object of evaluation: a completed argument whose consequential steps can be followed, challenged, and recombined in further work.

The dialogue agent thus neither simply abolishes Socrates's objection nor merely repeats it behind a speaking mask. It separates features that the opposition between speech and writing tends to bind together: adaptability, persistence, audience recognition, evidential support, and continuity of commitment. The role-play paper shows why a convincing conversational surface can leave one of those features absent, and its own proposed repair shows how an altered procedure can supply it. Grice clarifies the expectations that make such absences consequential. The Phaedrus supplies the larger demand that words become capable of sustaining further inquiry. An answer earns its place in that inquiry through the relations that let someone return to it, challenge it, and carry its reasons into another encounter. The unvarying page and the varying reply each have work to do.
