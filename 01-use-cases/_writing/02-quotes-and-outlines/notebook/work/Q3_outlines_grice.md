## Sketch 1 · The maxims written down

**Claim.** The assistant persona is Grice's Cooperative Principle made explicit and imposed from outside; what Grice treated as a tacit habit learned in childhood, the laboratory writes as instructions, and the writing-down changes what the maxims are.

**Crux.** p. 48: the "dull" answer, that people follow the maxims because "they have learned to do so in childhood and not lost the habit," set against Grice's wish for a basis that makes the practice "REASONABLE for us to follow." The paper has to show that both answers presuppose a speaker with a history, and that an assistant has neither the habit nor the reasons but only the rule.

**Collision.** Askell et al., *A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment* (2021), the paper the syllabus assigns for the chatbot session and describes as showing that "the familiar 'assistant' persona is itself something that was deliberately fabricated." It is known for framing the assistant as *helpful, honest, harmless* (*to verify* in the paper's own words and pages before citing). The question: are helpful, honest, harmless a restatement of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner, and if so, what happened to Manner, and where did "harmless" come from? Grice's p. 47 admission that information exchange is "too narrow" and that the scheme must allow for "influencing or directing the actions of others" is the door.

**Counter-reading.** Grice's maxims are not rules for a speaker but presumptions a hearer makes (p. 50: the general pattern is in the hearer's voice). Writing instructions for the speaker does not touch the hearer's presumption, so nothing Gricean has changed; the assistant is cooperative because we treat it so. The paper must answer with condition (3) on p. 50: the presumption includes what the speaker expects of the hearer.

**Shape.**
1. Open on p. 48, the four screws and the rubber spoon, and the sentence "It is much easier, for example, to tell the truth than to invent lies." Ask for whom that is true. Land the claim.
2. Read §§3–4: the maxims as a first approximation; the hedges (pp. 46–47); the search for a basis and its failure (pp. 48–49). Show that the essay's confidence is in the hearer's reasoning, not the speaker's rule.
3. Collide with Askell et al.: the fabricated assistant's instructions beside the four categories; what maps, what does not; "harmless" as a maxim Grice did not need because his speakers could "let down … themselves" (p. 49).
4. The counter-reading: hearers presume cooperation regardless. Answer: condition (3), and the testimonial on p. 52, where the implicature depends on knowing the writer "cannot be unable, through ignorance, to say more."
5. Return to the rubber spoon: a tool that looks like a spoon and is not one is Grice's example of a Quality failure in a transaction. End on what it means to be handed one by a system that has only ever been told to hand you spoons.

**Fit.** 4. The Grice side is fully in the bank; the Askell side needs a `Q1` run on that paper before anything is cited.

## Sketch 2 · Can a machine flout?

**Claim.** Conversational implicature, as Grice defines it, requires that the hearer presume the speaker could have complied with the maxim and chose not to; that presumption is exactly what a hearer cannot make, and does make, of a language model, which is why its ironies and refusals are read as role-play rather than as speech.

**Crux.** pp. 49–50, the three conditions, and p. 49 on flouting: "On the assumption that the speaker is able to fulfill the maxim and to do so without violating another maxim (because of a clash), is not opting out, and is not, in view of the blatancy of his performance, trying to mislead." The paper has to show that "is able to" and "is not opting out" are attributions of capacity and intention, and that the hearer makes them or does not.

**Collision.** Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds, "Role Play with Large Language Models" (2023), the required reading for Seminar 7, known for proposing that a model's apparent self is best described as a role it plays (*to verify*: the paper's own formulation and its examples). The question: when a hearer supplies conditions (1)–(3) for a model's output, is the hearer doing what Grice describes, or casting a role? Grice's irony example on p. 53 ("X is a fine friend") requires that "the audience knows that A knows that this is obvious to the audience": three layers of mutual knowledge.

**Counter-reading.** The garage example (p. 51): the implicature is worked out from the remark, the context, and the presumption, and nothing in the working-out mentions who spoke. Implicature is the hearer's achievement, so the speaker's nature is irrelevant. Answer: p. 50, "he knows (and knows that I know that he knows)": the pattern attributes knowledge to the speaker at every step.

**Shape.**
1. Open on the tea party (p. 54): "The weather has been quite delightful this summer, hasn't it?" Describe what B must be for the remark to be a rebuke. Land the claim.
2. Read §§5–6 closely: the four failures, the word "BLATANTLY," the three conditions, the pattern in the hearer's voice.
3. Collide with Shanahan et al.: role-play as the hearer's supply of conditions (1)–(3); the model as a speaker to whom "could have complied" is attributed by the one who listens.
4. The counter-reading from the garage: answer with the layered knowledge in the general pattern (p. 50) and with p. 58, the implicature carried "by the saying of what is said."
5. Return to the tea party with B as a machine. End on what "appalled silence" would mean.

**Fit.** 5. Both texts are short and both are on the syllabus; the Grice side is in the bank and the Shanahan side is a one-afternoon `Q1`.

## Sketch 3 · "My lips are sealed"

**Claim.** The chat interface enforces, by design, the third feature of Grice's quasi-contract, "You do not just shove off or start doing something else," so that a model's refusal can only ever be read as opting out, and opting out, in Grice's scheme, is the one failure that cannot generate an implicature without becoming a flout.

**Crux.** p. 48, the three features of a cooperative transaction and the sentence "You do not just shove off"; p. 49, OPT OUT, "I cannot say more; my lips are sealed." The paper has to show that Grice distinguishes opting out from flouting by what the speaker makes plain, and that an interface which never ends the exchange removes the speaker's ordinary way of making anything plain.

**Collision.** Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson, "A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation" (1974), the other Seminar 6 reading (*to verify*: its rules for turn allocation and closing). The question: Grice describes contribution and never turn; the chat window fixes the turn structure absolutely (one speaks, then the other, forever). What does a maxim mean inside a turn system no participant can leave?

**Counter-reading.** Grice himself rejects the quasi-contract (pp. 48–49): "there are too many types of exchange, like quarreling and letter writing, that it fails to fit comfortably." If the contract is not the basis of the maxims, an interface that enforces it proves nothing about them. Answer: the rejection is on grounds of fit, not of falsity; the interface is a case it fits perfectly, which is the oddity.

**Shape.**
1. Open on p. 49, "I cannot say more; my lips are sealed," and ask what a speaker who says this is allowed to do next. Land the claim.
2. Read §4: the quasi-contract, its three features, its rejection, and the sentence about letting down "himself."
3. Collide with turn-taking: the chat window as a turn system; opting out when the turn always returns.
4. The counter-reading: Grice's own rejection. Answer: fit versus falsity; the letter and the quarrel as exchanges one can leave.
5. Return to the sealed lips. End on the difference between a speaker who will not say more and a system that cannot stop.

**Fit.** 3. The idea is sharp but the Sacks side is a long, technical paper, and the claim about interfaces needs a documented example of a refusal (a published system prompt or model card would do; see `01-seminar-paper/notebook/W04_source-quotes.md`, Part C).
