# Q2 — Skeleton: Grice, "Logic and Conversation"

Reverse outline, from the full text and the `Q1` bank. Pages are the printed
pages 41–58 as marked in the course's Markdown.

## 1. The dispute, and the mistake both sides share (pp. 41–43)

- **Move.** Sets up a two-sided quarrel and then refuses to join it.
- **Content.** Logicians disagree about whether the formal devices (∼, ∧, ∨, ⊃, quantifiers, the definite description) diverge in meaning from *not*, *and*, *or*, *if*, *all*, *some*, *the*. The formalist treats the extra meaning of the natural words as "undesirable excrescences" and wants an ideal language; the informalist replies that language serves purposes besides science and that natural-language inferences are valid on their own terms. Grice: both assume the divergences exist; that assumption is the "common mistake," and it comes from "inadequate attention to the nature and importance of the conditions governing conversation."
- **Anchor.** p. 43, *crux*.

## 2. Implicature as a term of art (pp. 43–45)

- **Move.** Introduces the vocabulary by an example, then fixes what "say" will mean.
- **Content.** B says C "hasn't been to prison yet"; what B implied is distinct from what B said. *Implicate*, *implicature*, *implicatum* are coined to cover the whole family of implying, suggesting, meaning. "Say" is tied closely to the conventional meaning of the sentence, though full identification needs reference, time, and disambiguation ("in the grip of a vice"). Some implicatures are conventional ("He is an Englishman; he is, therefore, brave"); the essay is about the nonconventional kind that depend on "general features of discourse."
- **Anchor.** p. 43, *example* and *definition*.

## 3. The Cooperative Principle and the four categories (pp. 45–47)

- **Move.** States a principle, then a scheme under it, then ranks the parts and admits the scheme is too narrow.
- **Content.** Talk exchanges are cooperative efforts with an accepted purpose or direction; hence the Cooperative Principle. "Echoing Kant," four categories: Quantity (as informative as required; not more), Quality (supermaxim: try to be truthful; do not say what you believe false; do not say what you lack evidence for), Relation ("Be relevant," which "conceals a number of problems"), Manner (be perspicuous: avoid obscurity, ambiguity, prolixity, disorder; "And one might need others"). Not all maxims are equally urgent; the first maxim of Quality may be prior to the scheme. Other maxims exist ("Be polite"). The information-exchange purpose "is, of course, too narrow."
- **Anchor.** p. 45, Cooperative Principle; pp. 45–46, the maxims; p. 47, the *objection*.

## 4. Why follow the principle at all? (pp. 47–49)

- **Move.** Asks for a basis, gives the easy answer, wants a better one, tries one, and leaves the question open.
- **Content.** Analogies with non-talk transactions (four screws, not two or six; sugar not salt; no rubber spoon). The "dull" answer: people do behave this way, learned in childhood; "It is much easier, for example, to tell the truth than to invent lies." The rationalist wish: to show the practice is "REASONABLE for us to follow." A quasi-contract is tried, with three features (common immediate aim; dovetailed contributions; "You do not just shove off"), and found not to fit "quarreling and letter writing"; besides, the obscure talker "has primarily let down not his audience but himself." The hoped-for argument from anyone who cares about communication's goals is stated and deferred: "I am uncertain."
- **Anchor.** p. 48, the two *turns*; pp. 48–49, *objection*.

## 5. Four ways to fail a maxim (p. 49)

- **Move.** Lists, so that one item can be singled out.
- **Content.** Violate (quietly; may mislead). Opt out ("my lips are sealed"). Clash (one maxim against another). Flout (blatantly), which, given that the speaker could comply, is not opting out, and is not trying to mislead, leaves the hearer a problem to solve: this is where conversational implicature arises, and the maxim is "EXPLOITED."
- **Anchor.** p. 49, both *definitions*.

## 6. The definition, and the working-out (pp. 49–50)

- **Move.** Defines, then insists the thing defined must be calculable, then gives the calculation's data and its general pattern.
- **Content.** Three conditions: the speaker is presumed to observe the maxims or at least the CP; the supposition that *q* is needed to keep *p* consistent with that presumption; the speaker thinks the hearer can work this out and expects the hearer to think so. Applied to the prison example. An implicature that cannot be "worked out" is conventional, not conversational. Data: conventional meaning and references; the CP and maxims; context; background; and the mutual availability of all of these. The general pattern in the hearer's voice ("He has said that *p* … and so he has implicated that *q*").
- **Anchor.** pp. 49–50, *crux*; p. 50, *claim* and *definition*.

## 7. Examples in three groups (pp. 50–56)

- **Move.** Illustrates, from the plainest case to figures of speech.
- **Content.** Group A, no maxim violated (the garage round the corner; visits to New York). Group B, a clash (somewhere in the South of France: Quantity yields to Quality). Group C, exploitation: the testimonial that praises English and attendance; tautologies ("War is war"); over-information; irony ("X is a fine friend"), metaphor ("the cream in my coffee"), meiosis, hyperbole; the second Quality maxim ("She is probably deceiving him this evening"); real irrelevance (the tea party); deliberate ambiguity (Blake; *Peccavi*); deliberate obscurity (in front of a child); prolixity (Miss X and the score of "Home sweet home").
- **Anchor.** pp. 52–55, the *examples* and *jokes*.

## 8. Particularized and generalized implicature (pp. 56–57)

- **Move.** Distinguishes, then argues against multiplying senses.
- **Content.** Some implicatures depend on the occasion; some are carried "normally" by a form of words ("X is meeting a woman this evening"; "a house … a tortoise"). Rather than three senses of "an X," the implicature is a failure to be specific where specificity was expected, under the first maxim of Quantity. Why specificity is expected: transactions with one's own things differ from transactions with others'; "Information, like money, is often given without the giver's knowing to just what use the recipient will want to put it."
- **Anchor.** p. 56, *definition*; p. 57, *image*.

## 9. Five features of conversational implicature (pp. 57–58)

- **Move.** Derives consequences from the definition.
- **Content.** Cancelable (explicitly or by context, because one can opt out). Nondetachable (another way of saying the same thing keeps it, unless Manner is involved). Not part of the expression's meaning, initially at least. Carried "by the saying of what is said, or by 'putting it that way,'" not by what is said. Indeterminate, because the list of explanations may be open.
- **Anchor.** p. 57, *claim*; p. 58, both *claims*.

## The spine

1. The logicians' quarrel about *and*, *or*, *if* rests on a shared mistake about what conversation adds to meaning (§1).
2. What conversation adds is implicature, distinct from what is said (§2).
3. Implicature is possible because talk is cooperative: a principle and four categories of maxims, offered as a first approximation (§3), whose rational basis Grice wants and cannot yet give (§4).
4. A conversational implicature is what a hearer must suppose to keep the speaker cooperative when a maxim is flouted, and it must be workable-out (§§5–6), as the examples show (§7).
5. Hence its properties: cancelable, nondetachable, not meaning, carried by the saying, indeterminate (§§8–9).

## The hinges

- **p. 47.** The maxims are stated for information exchange, which Grice calls "too narrow"; the scheme "needs to be generalized to allow for such general purposes as influencing or directing the actions of others." The essay never does the generalizing. A paper about instruction-following systems can.
- **pp. 48–49.** The basis for the principle: the "dull" habit answer versus the rationalist wish, with the quasi-contract tried and rejected. Grice ends in uncertainty. Any speaker who did not learn to talk in childhood, and cannot "let down … himself," puts pressure exactly here.
- **p. 49, condition (3) on p. 50.** Exploitation requires that the speaker could have complied, is not opting out, and expects the hearer to work it out. Whether a hearer can presume this of a non-human speaker, and whether the presumption is enough, is the question the definition leaves to the hearer.

## What is not here

- No treatment of relevance, which Grice defers twice (pp. 46, 49).
- No account of politeness beyond one mention (p. 47).
- No turn-taking: the exchange is described by what is contributed, not by who speaks when. Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson (1974), the other Seminar 6 reading, supply what Grice leaves out.
- No speaker who is not a person. Every example has a man, a reviewer, a general, a poet.
