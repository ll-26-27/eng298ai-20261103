Across the 21 renderings we could verify (14 languages, 1821 to 2025), one choice holds without exception: every translator keeps **Adam** by name and keeps a **fallen angel**. The frame around that agreement varies. Bonillo insists (*sou mesmo é teu anjo caído*, "what I really am is your fallen angel"); Göncz makes it a likeness (*olyan vagyok, mint a bukott angyal*, "I am like the fallen angel"), Alemany an appearance (*parecer el ángel caído*), and the unnamed 2003 French a diminishment (*je ne suis qu'un ange déchu*, "I am only a fallen angel"). The two Chinese versions move the angel: Liu Xinmin's is "cast into hell" (被打入地狱的天使), the unidentified translator's "banished to the lower world" (被贬谪下界的天使).

The sharp split is **fiend**. Twelve renderings keep a demon or devil: *demonio* (Pareja, Torres Oliver), *demone* (Maghelli), *demônio* (Miranda), *zum Teufel* (the Grawes), *злобного демона*, "a malicious demon" (Aleksandrova, who adds the malice), *демон* (Georgieva), *démont* (Göncz), *duivel* (Brinkman), *şeytan* (Seber), 恶魔 (the unidentified Chinese), and Shishido's 鬼, the *oni* or ogre-demon of Japanese folklore, which fits "devil" only loosely. Two make him a monster (Alemany's *monstruo*, the 2003 French *monstre*). Five dissolve the noun into an adjective or a paraphrase, so that the creature becomes bad rather than a being of another order: Widtmann's *das Unglück hat mich zu dem gemacht, was ich jetzt bin* ("misfortune has made me what I now am"), Korbař's *zlým* ("evil"), Stevović's *rđavim* ("wicked", a word cognate with "rusty"), Liu's 凶残歹毒 ("vicious and wicked"), and the unnamed Spanish *me ha envilecido* ("has debased me"). Two stand alone: Saladin's *semblable au génie du mal* ("like the spirit of evil"), a simile that keeps the creature at one remove, and Bonillo's *inimigo*, "enemy", which turns a devil into an adversary.

Around that noun, many translators tilt the creature toward pity. **Benevolent** becomes meekness or a child's good behaviour: *кроток* and *кротък* (Russian and Bulgarian "meek", the word of the Beatitudes in Russian), *hodný* (Czech), Seber's *uslu* (Turkish "well-behaved", the opposite of a naughty child, in the national dictionary), *cariñoso*, *afectuoso*, *bonaria*. **Misery** usually becomes something that happened to him, misfortune or fate, rather than a state he is in: *malheur*, *Unglück*, *desgracia*, *несчастья*, *nevolja*, *злата участ* ("evil fate"), 不幸. Only a few keep wretchedness itself: Göncz's *nyomorúság*, Bonillo's *miséria*, Brinkman's *Ellende*.

On the biblical and secular axis, the most consistent move toward scripture is to name **Paradise** where Shelley has only "joy": Widtmann (*aus dem Paradies vertreibst*), the Grawes (*aus dem Paradies verstößt*), Miranda (*expulsa do paraíso*) and Liu (逐出天国的乐园, "drove out of the paradise of the heavenly kingdom"). Aleksandrova's *отлучил* can carry the sense of excommunication, though the dictionary shows it is not limited to it. The secularizing moves cluster on **misdeed**. Three translators drop it (Widtmann, the unnamed Spanish, the 2003 French). Four move the injustice from the creature's innocence to Victor's arbitrariness: the Grawes' *grundlos* ("without reason"), Brinkman's *op onredelijke wijze* ("unreasonably"), and the two Chinese idioms 平白无故 and 无缘无故 ("for no reason at all"). Two make it a crime: Saladin's *forfait* (a grave, monstrous crime, in the French dictionary) and Alemany's *delito*. Only Miranda (*pecado*) and Stevović (*zgrešio*, built on *greh*, "sin") make it a sin.

These are not patterns of period or language: most languages here have one or two renderings and six are undated. Where we have pairs, they cut against tidy stories. The two Germans (1912, 1986) both add Paradise but split on "fiend". The two Brazilians, two years apart, sit at opposite poles. The Slavic translations of the 1960s divide between a heightened demon and a flattened adjective. What does recur, across languages and decades, is the softening of "benevolent" toward meekness or docility and the externalizing of "misery" as bad luck: translators more often make the creature a wronged child than a self-made devil.

### Four contrasts worth reading side by side

> **Two Brazilian versions, two years apart.** Bonillo (2023): *desvias da alegria por nenhum malfeito … a miséria fez de mim um inimigo* ("you turn me from joy for no wrongdoing … misery made me an enemy"). Miranda (2025): *expulsa do paraíso sem nenhum pecado … a tristeza fez de mim um demônio* ("expel from paradise without any sin … sadness made me a demon"). Same language, same decade: one secular adversary, one fully scriptural demon.

> **The 1960s Slavic translations.** Aleksandrova (1965) adds malice, *злобного демона*; Korbař (1966, *mě učinilo zlým*, "made me evil") and Stevović (1960, *me je učinila rđavim*, "made me wicked") reduce the demon to a moral adjective. Within a few years and a related family of languages, one translator heightens the demonic and two turn damnation into character.

> **Whose fault?** Pareja's *sin que haya cometido falta alguna* and Miranda's *sem nenhum pecado* keep the creature's claim to be guiltless. The Grawes' *grundlos*, Brinkman's *op onredelijke wijze* and Liu's 平白无故 instead judge Victor's act as unreasonable. The moral centre of the accusation shifts from the accused to the accuser.

> **Insisting or hedging.** Bonillo insists (*sou mesmo é teu anjo caído*); Göncz (*olyan vagyok, mint*), Alemany (*parecer*) and the 2003 French (*je ne suis qu'un*) turn identity into likeness, appearance or diminishment. Shelley's "I am rather the fallen angel" is a defiant self-placement; how firmly it is kept decides whether the creature reads as Satanic or merely wronged.

**Before trusting any of this,** check the quotations (only Saladin's and Widtmann's are full, public-domain passages; Brinkman's is partial; Aleksandrova's carries an OCR slip), the editions and attributions (three translators are unnamed, six renderings undated), and the connotation claims: the ones we looked up are listed below, and the rest are a model's readings.
