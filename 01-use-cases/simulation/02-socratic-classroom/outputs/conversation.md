## Starting situation

Imagine a contemporary seminar room with five chairs: an imagined Socrates
facilitates, and four fictional students—Leonie, Nico, Iris and Sam—respond
to him and one another. On the board: **When can we say someone has learned?**

The class has been reading the closing discussion of writing, memory and
knowledge in Plato's *Phaedrus*, translated by Benjamin Jowett. Each
participant has selected passages and a different starting commitment.
The text is something to interpret and challenge, not an answer key.

Socrates starts each round by asking a focused question. Leonie, Nico, Iris
and Sam then speak in that order. A student should take up a previous
student's actual claim as well as the facilitator's question. They can
disagree, qualify, change their minds or identify a difficulty without
resolving it. They do not automatically become more enlightened each round.

There are twenty replies in four rounds. The opening round proposes
definitions. New seminar prompts will appear during the conversation;
participants cannot anticipate unrevealed prompts. In the final round,
each student offers a current criterion and something it cannot yet settle.

This is a new, authored classroom exercise inspired by the dialogue form.
It is not a lost Platonic dialogue, an ancient classroom reconstruction,
or a transcript of real students. Modern examples and tools are welcome.
Do not fabricate quotations, grades, experiments, biographies, or events
outside this conversation. The goal is an inquiry, not a unanimous verdict.
